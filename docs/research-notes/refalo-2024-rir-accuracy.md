## Evidence type

Randomized crossover trial (2 experimental sessions, ~48 h apart), peer-reviewed (Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research).

## Research question

How accurate are resistance-trained individuals' intraset repetitions-in-reserve (RIR) predictions during the barbell bench press, when tested against actual repetitions performed to momentary muscular failure — and does this accuracy differ between a 1-RIR vs. 3-RIR target, between sets, between sessions, or by biological sex, training experience, or relative strength?

## Population and evidence base

- 24 resistance-trained subjects (12 male, 12 female).
- ≥3 years resistance-training experience required for inclusion; mean experience 8.3±3.7 y (men), 7.2±2.3 y (women).
- "Advanced" training status operationalized via relative bench press strength: men 1.37±0.26 kg/kg body mass (>120% BW threshold), women 0.88±0.17 kg/kg body mass (>80% BW threshold).
- Randomized crossover design: each subject completed 2 sets to momentary muscular failure on barbell bench press at 75% 1RM, once per session across 2 sessions ~48 h apart; order of 1-RIR vs. 3-RIR prediction randomized.

## Variables investigated

- Protocol: subjects performed sets to momentary muscular failure at 75% 1RM on barbell bench press; during each set, subjects verbally called out when they believed they had reached a 1-RIR point (one set) and a 3-RIR point (the other set), with prediction order randomized. Subjects were not counting reps aloud or receiving rep-count feedback during the set.
- RIR accuracy = predicted RIR at the called-out point vs. actual RIR (reps remaining until failure, counted post hoc from the completed set).
- Both **raw accuracy** (signed error; negative = underestimation of RIR, i.e., subject believed fewer reps remained than actually did) and **absolute accuracy** (unsigned magnitude of error) were computed.
- Comparisons: 1-RIR vs. 3-RIR predictions; set 1 vs. set 2 (order effect); session 1 vs. session 2 (learning/familiarization effect); statistical equivalence testing used for all three comparisons (equivalence range/smallest-effect-size-of-interest = ±1 repetition).
- Exploratory multiple regression: RIR accuracy (both raw and absolute) regressed on biological sex, years of RT experience, and relative bench press strength.

## Main findings

- **Combined raw RIR accuracy (1-RIR and 3-RIR pooled):** −0.17±1.00 reps — a slight overall underprediction trend (subjects tended to slightly underestimate how many reps remained, i.e., they on average called "RIR" slightly before the true failure-relative point would suggest, per the paper's sign convention).
- **Combined absolute RIR accuracy:** 0.65±0.78 reps (range 0–3) — on average, predictions were within well under 1 repetition of the true target.
- **1-RIR predictions:** raw −0.23±0.75 reps [95% CI −0.52, 0.06]; absolute 0.40±0.68 reps (range 0–3).
- **3-RIR predictions:** raw −0.10±1.21 reps [95% CI −0.45, 0.14]; absolute 0.90±0.81 reps (range 0–3) — more absolute error at 3-RIR (farther from failure) than at 1-RIR (closer to failure), consistent with the general "accuracy is better closer to failure" pattern found elsewhere in this corpus (e.g., `bastos-rir.md`, EVIDENCE-014; and the secondary accuracy data in paper 13/`refalo-2024-rir-vs-failure.md`).
- **Statistical equivalence testing (±1 repetition equivalence range):**
  - Raw accuracy: 1-RIR vs. 3-RIR equivalent (−0.08 reps [90% CI −0.42, 0.26]); set 1 vs. set 2 equivalent (0.12 reps [90% CI −0.31, 0.56]); session 1 vs. session 2 equivalent (0.51 reps [90% CI 0.16, 0.85] — this CI does not cross zero, indicating a detectable directional difference toward greater underprediction in session 2, but both CI bounds still fall within the ±1 rep equivalence range, so the difference is deemed statistically equivalent under the pre-specified criterion despite being non-zero).
  - Absolute accuracy: 1-RIR vs. 3-RIR equivalent (−0.54 [90% CI −0.85, −0.24]); set 1 vs. set 2 equivalent (−0.13 [−0.44, 0.18]); session 1 vs. session 2 equivalent (0.11 [−0.18, 0.41]).
- **Exploratory regression on accuracy predictors:** raw accuracy model R²=0.22, adjusted R²=0.15 (weak explanatory power; predictors = sex + years RT experience); absolute accuracy model R²=0.08, adjusted R²=0.00 (negligible explanatory power; predictors = sex + relative bench press strength). No meaningful relationship detected between RIR accuracy and years of training experience, biological sex, or relative bench press strength in this sample.
- **Contrast with some prior literature:** this study found comparable accuracy between 1-RIR and 3-RIR and between sessions, which contrasts with some earlier work suggesting accuracy improves closer to failure or with repeated exposure. The authors attribute this methodologically: this study let subjects call out their RIR estimate at any point they chose during the set, whereas some prior studies required a prediction at a fixed, pre-specified repetition number (e.g., always predict at rep 10), which can artificially bias predictions to look more accurate as fatigue accumulates near a fixed checkpoint.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

Not applicable — this paper does not measure or report strength or hypertrophy outcomes. It is a pure measurement-validity/psychophysical-accuracy study.

## Practical interpretation

Resistance-trained individuals with substantial training experience (mean >7 years) and above-average relative bench press strength demonstrated fairly high absolute RIR accuracy — on average within about half a repetition (1-RIR) to under one repetition (3-RIR) of the true target — with a modest overall tendency toward slight underprediction (calling failure slightly early relative to the true RIR point). Accuracy did not differ meaningfully by target RIR value, set order, or session in this study (all statistically equivalent within ±1 rep), and no significant predictors of individual accuracy (sex, experience, relative strength) emerged in exploratory analysis. This supports RIR as a **usably accurate**, not perfectly precise, self-report tool in trained lifters: real-world RIR reports should be expected to carry roughly ±1 rep of typical noise even among experienced, strong trainees performing a single well-practiced barbell exercise under controlled test conditions — and this is likely a best-case accuracy scenario (single exercise, no concurrent rep-counting/pacing demands, motivated experienced subjects) rather than a representative floor for accuracy in general training conditions.

## Application relevance

This paper is the corpus's most direct evidence on RIR *measurement accuracy* itself (as distinct from evidence on RIR as a *training variable/target*, covered by other papers). It is directly relevant to any product decision to collect user-reported RIR values and to any future automated logic that would consume those values. Two distinct claims must be kept separate: **(1) "RIR is useful enough to track"** — supported, since accuracy in this study was good enough (typically well under 1 rep of error) to be practically informative; and **(2) "RIR is an objectively precise measurement"** — NOT supported, since a real, non-zero (~0.4–0.9 rep average absolute) error exists even under best-case conditions (experienced, strong subjects, single practiced exercise, no distractions), with a directional tendency toward underprediction that grew slightly in session 2. Any recommendation or progression logic that treats a user-entered RIR value as an exact, noiseless number is not supported by this evidence — the data instead support treating RIR as a **useful but noisy signal**, meriting tolerance for roughly ±1 rep of typical error in downstream logic design (an architectural/data-modeling implication, not a specific algorithmic rule).

## What this paper DOES support

- Resistance-trained individuals (mean >7 years experience, above-average relative strength) can predict RIR during the bench press with fairly high absolute accuracy — typically under 1 repetition of average error.
- A modest overall tendency toward RIR underprediction (i.e., subjects on average called their RIR target slightly earlier than the true failure-relative point).
- No statistically meaningful accuracy difference between a 1-RIR and 3-RIR target, between the first and second set, or between the first and second session, in this specific tested population and protocol (all differences fell within the pre-specified ±1 rep equivalence range).
- RIR accuracy, in this sample, was not clearly predicted by biological sex, years of training experience, or relative bench press strength (though the regression models had weak-to-negligible explanatory power, so this is a limited null finding, not strong evidence of "no such relationship exists").
- RIR should be treated as a useful but imperfect (noisy) self-report signal, not an exact measurement.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- Does NOT establish that RIR accuracy is perfect or that a reported RIR value should be treated as exact.
- Does NOT generalize beyond the bench press to other exercises or muscle groups — single-exercise study.
- Does NOT establish accuracy patterns for untrained, novice, or intermediate lifters — all subjects were highly experienced (mean >7 years) and above-average strength; the authors themselves note RIR prescriptions "may be most suitable for resistance-trained individuals," implying accuracy for less experienced populations is unknown from this data.
- Does NOT reflect real-world training conditions where lifters are often simultaneously counting/pacing their own repetitions — this study specifically had subjects not counting or receiving rep feedback during the set, which may make its accuracy figures a best-case rather than typical-case estimate.
- Does NOT support a specific numeric error-tolerance threshold as a designed product feature (e.g., "always apply a ±1 rep correction") — it supports general architectural tolerance for RIR noise, not a specific correction algorithm.
- Does NOT establish whether accuracy is better or worse specifically as failure is approached within a single set beyond the two discrete points tested (1-RIR, 3-RIR) — no continuous accuracy curve across all RIR values was measured.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
1. Single exercise (barbell bench press only) — generalization to other exercises/muscle groups is unknown and plausibly variable (e.g., accuracy may differ for multi-joint lower-body lifts, isolation exercises, or exercises with different fatigue/technical-breakdown profiles).
2. Repetitions were not counted aloud by subjects nor reported to researchers during the set, so whether accuracy holds when lifters are actively counting/pacing (common in real training and app-based logging) is unknown.
3. Sample is highly experienced (mean RT experience >7 years, above-average relative strength) — accuracy may not generalize to novice or intermediate trainees.
4. Only two discrete RIR targets tested (1 and 3) — the full accuracy curve across the RIR range (0 through, e.g., 5+) was not assessed.

**Agent-assessed (not stated by authors):**
- The session 2 raw-accuracy CI not crossing zero (while still falling within the ±1 rep equivalence bound) suggests a subtle systematic shift toward greater underprediction with repeated exposure that the equivalence-testing framework classifies as "practically equivalent" but which is nonetheless a directional signal worth not fully discarding — small, repeated systematic biases could compound over a long-term logging history even if trivial in any single session.
- This is a secondary analysis drawing on the same parent data-collection effort referenced in paper 13's methods ("pretesting phase") — while it is a distinct, standalone published study, the subject pool's overlap with the broader Refalo/Helms/Fyfe research program (which also produced paper 13) means this is not a fully independent replication from an unrelated lab; corroboration from a genuinely separate research group would strengthen confidence further.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** Randomized crossover design with formal statistical equivalence testing (a methodologically stronger approach than simple null-hypothesis testing for establishing "no meaningful difference," since it directly bounds the plausible effect size rather than merely failing to reject a null). Single peer-reviewed study, single exercise, single lab/research-program lineage, and a narrow, highly experienced population — sufficient for the general "RIR carries real but modest, practically-tolerable error, even among experts" conclusion, but not sufficient alone to establish precise, generalizable numeric accuracy figures across exercises or training-experience levels.

This paper is the most methodologically rigorous of the three specifically for the accuracy question (dedicated randomized crossover design with formal equivalence testing), while `bastos-rir.md` provides broader scoping-review context across more studies/populations, and paper 13's Table 3 provides a smaller, incidental accuracy measurement embedded within a longer training intervention. None of the three establishes RIR as a precise, error-free measurement — all support "useful but noisy," which should inform how confidently any future feature treats a logged RIR value.
