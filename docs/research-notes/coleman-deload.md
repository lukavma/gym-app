# Citation

Coleman M, Burke R, Augustin F, Piñero A, Maldonado J, Fisher JP, Israetel M, Androulakis Korakakis P, Swinton P, Oberlin D, Schoenfeld BJ. 2024. Gaining more from doing less? The effects of a one-week deload period during supervised resistance training on muscular adaptations. *PeerJ* 12:e16777. DOI: 10.7717/peerj.16777. Published 22 January 2024.

Source PDF:
`07_coleman_deload.pdf`

## Evidence type

Randomized controlled trial (parallel-group, two-arm), Bayesian statistical analysis. This is an **experimental intervention study**, not a survey, narrative review, or observational study. Prospectively registered (OSF), CONSORT-reported, with blinded outcome assessment (ultrasound technician and statistician blinded to group allocation).

## Research question

Whether a 1-week deload period (complete cessation of resistance training) inserted at the midpoint of a 9-week resistance training (RT) program "potentiates" (enhances) subsequent muscular adaptations in resistance-trained individuals, via a hypothesized "re-sensitization" mechanism, compared to continuous training over the same period.

## Population and evidence base

- Single RCT, one cohort. Not a meta-analysis or survey of practices.
- 50 male and female university-recruited volunteers randomized; 39 completed (DELOAD n=18: 12 male, 6 female; TRAD n=21: 17 male, 4 female).
- Age: DELOAD 22.2 ± 6.1 yrs; TRAD 21.4 ± 3.9 yrs (inclusion range 18–40 yrs).
- Training status: "resistance-trained," defined as lifting ≥3x/week (most weeks), with ≥1 weekly lower-body session, for ≥1 year. Training experience: DELOAD 3.7 ± 4.5 yrs, TRAD 3.2 ± 2.6 yrs.
- Duration: 9-week program; DELOAD group abstained entirely from RT during week 5 (allowed aerobic/sport-specific training); TRAD trained continuously.
- Training: lower body directly supervised, 2x/week (Smith squat, leg extension, straight-leg toe press, seated calf raise; 5 sets of 8–12RM to volitional failure, 2 min rest). Upper body was unsupervised/self-directed 2x/week (mandatory but adherence/log quality was inconsistent — noted as a limitation). Total volume across all muscle groups combined: 90 sets/week.
- Attendance among completers: >85% of sessions, ~96% average both groups. Dropouts (11/50, 22%): personal reasons (n=5), non-compliance (n=5), unrelated injury (n=1).
- Funding/COI: funded by Renaissance Periodization, LLC, which had a role in study design and manuscript preparation; co-author Michael Israetel is a co-founder of Renaissance Periodization; co-author Brad Schoenfeld sits on Tonal Corporation's scientific advisory board. (RP's proprietary terminology/framework is not treated as evidence here per task instructions; this note only reports the paper's directly measured RCT outcomes.)

## Variables investigated

- Deload type tested: **one single protocol only** — complete cessation of lower-body RT for exactly 1 week, pre-planned, positioned at the midpoint (after week 4) of a 9-week block. No other deload duration, frequency, timing, or "reduced-volume/load" style deload was tested.
- Outcomes: muscle thickness (ultrasound; quadriceps at 30/50/70% sites, medial/lateral gastrocnemius, soleus), body fat %, lower-body lean mass (BIA), isometric knee-extension strength (dynamometry), dynamic strength (1RM Smith squat), local muscular endurance (leg-extension reps at 60% body mass), lower-body power (countermovement jump), and a secondary "readiness to train" questionnaire (soreness, motivation, sleep quality, etc.) plus dietary intake.

## Main findings

- **Body composition / muscle morphology (hypertrophy):** No appreciable between-group differences. 95% credible intervals substantially overlapped zero for all muscle thickness sites and body fat/lean mass; posterior probabilities favoring the deload group's inclusion ranged 0.273–0.835 across sites (Table 1/2). Both groups showed small-to-medium positive within-group increases in muscle thickness; body fat % and lower-body lean mass showed minimal change in both groups.
- **Dynamic strength (1RM Smith squat):** TRAD improved more than DELOAD. Univariate posterior probability favoring inclusion of the deload = 0.149 (i.e., 85.1% probability favoring TRAD); multivariate = 0.116. Group-difference CI for effect size spanned a modest negative to an appreciable positive effect favoring TRAD (approx. −3.0 to 12.1 kg). Raw values: DELOAD 92.8±38.5 → 105.8±32.1 kg; TRAD 95.9±21.7 → 112.3±21.3 kg.
- **Isometric strength (knee extension):** TRAD improved more than DELOAD. Multivariate posterior probability favoring deload = 0.076 (i.e., 92.4% probability favoring TRAD). Effect-size CI spanned small-negative to large-positive favoring TRAD (approx. −5.1 to 42.1 N·m). Raw values: DELOAD 258.8±60.6 → 261.8±70.5 N·m; TRAD 268.4±55.0 → 288.6±55.0 N·m.
- **Local muscular endurance (leg extension reps):** Slightly favored DELOAD, but the between-group difference was less than 1 repetition (univariate diff −0.55 [95% CrI −2.9 to 1.9], p=0.321) — authors state this is "not likely to be of practical significance."
- **Power (countermovement jump):** Trivial between-group difference (diff 0.61 cm [95% CrI −1.5 to 2.8], p=0.715).
- **Secondary/readiness-to-train:** No substantial evidence of a between-group difference in nutritional intake. Some evidence of greater sleep quality in DELOAD at mid-intervention. Some evidence of greater muscle soreness in DELOAD at post-intervention. DELOAD reported a decrease in motivation to train from week 4 to week 9; TRAD reported no change in motivation. Authors describe these readiness differences as "relatively modest" with "questionable" practical meaningfulness.
- **Qualitative/anecdotal (not formally inferential):** Participants in DELOAD reported feeling "lethargic (i.e., out of practice)" after the deload rather than refreshed. At study end, virtually all participants across both groups reported training harder than at any prior point in their training history; almost none felt they needed a break; nearly all said they would return to normal training within a couple of days.
- No markers of fatigue or anabolic signaling (e.g., molecular/hormonal) were measured, so the "re-sensitization" mechanism motivating the study was not directly tested — only its downstream outcomes were.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

The paper explicitly separates these outcome domains and finds **different results for each**:
- **Hypertrophy (muscle thickness):** No difference between a 1-week complete-cessation deload and continuous training over the 9-week block — deload neither helped nor hurt muscle growth in this design.
- **Maximal strength (dynamic 1RM and isometric):** Continuous training outperformed the deload group; the deload appears to have blunted strength gains specifically.
- **Local muscular endurance:** Essentially unaffected (trivial, sub-1-rep difference).
- **Power (CMJ):** Essentially unaffected (trivial difference), consistent with prior literature that short (<2 week) detraining periods don't meaningfully affect power.
- No cardiovascular/aerobic endurance outcome was assessed (only local muscular endurance, a resistance-training-specific outcome, not aerobic capacity).
- This is outcome data from a controlled experiment, not a descriptive/practice-survey paper — but note it is a single trial, and the strength finding in particular is described by the authors themselves as unexpected given deloads are common practice in strength/power sports.

## Practical interpretation

Within this specific ecologically-motivated design (young resistance-trained lifters, high-volume 8–12RM hypertrophy-oriented lower-body program, one week of *complete* training cessation at the midpoint of a 9-week block), taking a full week completely off training did not enhance or impair hypertrophy, power, or local muscular endurance, but was associated with smaller gains in maximal strength (both dynamic and isometric) relative to training straight through. The authors' own interpretation: when the goal is to maximize hypertrophy, periodic (deloaded) and continuous training blocks are both viable over a ~9-week span; when the goal is to maximize maximal strength, periods of complete training cessation should likely be used more sparingly. The authors also speculate — without directly testing it — that a reduced-volume/reduced-intensity deload (rather than complete cessation) as more typically used by coaches/athletes in the field might behave differently (e.g., dissipating fatigue without causing the "lethargy"/detraining effect observed here), but this was not tested.

## Application relevance

- Trackable variable this paper speaks to: whether a "deload week" is implemented as complete training cessation vs. some reduced-volume/load alternative, and its differential effect on tracked strength (1RM/isometric) vs. hypertrophy (girth/thickness) trends.
- Recommendation signal (weak, single-study): a heuristic seed that a full rest-week deload is unlikely to detectably slow hypertrophy progress over a short (~9-week) block, but may coincide with a temporary dip or plateau in strength-test performance (1RM) immediately following the deload — this could inform how an app interprets/flags a strength-metric dip after a user-logged full-rest week, rather than assuming regression or program failure.
- Not established: any specific deload frequency, duration, or timing rule. The study tested exactly one deload length (1 week) at exactly one position (midpoint of a 9-week block) — there is no dose-response data here to seed rules like "deload every N weeks" or "deload for N days."
- Not suitable for automated decision-making beyond flagging this single trade-off (possible strength dip, no clear hypertrophy effect) for complete-rest-style deloads specifically; the paper explicitly does not test reduced-volume/load deload strategies, which are what most real-world coaches reportedly use.

## What this paper DOES support

- In young, resistance-trained (≥1 yr experience) men and women, a single 1-week *complete cessation* of lower-body RT at the midpoint of a 9-week, high-volume (90 sets/week combined), moderate-load (8–12RM to failure) program produced muscle thickness gains statistically indistinguishable from continuous training over the same period.
- The same complete-cessation deload was associated with smaller gains in lower-body maximal strength (1RM squat and isometric knee extension) than continuous training, with posterior probabilities of ~85–92% favoring the continuously-trained group.
- The complete-cessation deload did not meaningfully change countermovement jump power or local muscular endurance versus continuous training.
- This is causal/experimental evidence (within the bounds of one RCT) for these specific comparisons, not merely a description of common practice.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- That deloading in general — of any duration, frequency, or style — improves training outcomes. This study found no hypertrophy benefit and a possible strength detriment from the one deload protocol tested.
- A universal optimal deload frequency or duration; only one protocol (1 week, complete cessation, at week 5 of 9) was tested, with no comparison across durations/frequencies/timings.
- That a one-week deload is superior (or inferior) to deloads of other lengths — no other lengths were compared.
- That reduced-volume/reduced-load "in the field" style deloads (the kind coaches reportedly use, per the introduction's citation of other literature) behave the same as the complete-cessation deload tested here — the authors explicitly flag this as untested and a key difference from real-world practice.
- Generalization beyond young (18–40 yr), previously-trained lifters without Smith-machine-specific experience; beyond ~9-week training blocks; beyond ~90 sets/week volume and 2x/week per-muscle frequency; or to upper-body musculature (not directly measured/supervised).
- Any claim about autoregulated (need-based) deloads — only a pre-planned deload was tested.
- Any mechanistic claim about "re-sensitization" or anabolic signaling — no molecular/hormonal markers were measured; this hypothesis motivated the study but was not directly tested.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- Findings may not generalize to individuals over 40, adolescents, or untrained individuals (sample was young, previously trained).
- Participants were not required to have prior Smith-machine squat experience specifically, so 1RM strength gains/losses may partly reflect neural/skill (motor learning) adaptations rather than pure strength adaptations, limiting generalization to lifters already experienced with that exact movement.
- Some participants ended sets before true momentary failure despite verbal encouragement, though authors judge this had little effect on results since all trained with high effort.
- Outcomes were lower-body only; upper-body training was unsupervised and self-logged with inconsistent reporting quality, so no inference about deloading's effect on upper-body muscle can be drawn.
- Only a pre-planned deload after a 4-week cycle was tested; effects of deloads after longer training cycles, or autoregulated (need-based) deloads, are unknown.
- Findings are specific to a short (9-week), high-volume (90 weekly sets) block with each muscle trained only 2x/week; effects with longer periods or different volumes/frequencies are unknown.
- No markers of anabolic signaling were measured, precluding direct insight into the "re-sensitization" mechanism that motivated the study.
- No time-matched inactive control group was used (deemed infeasible to recruit trained individuals to fully cease training for ~10 weeks); the TRAD group served as the de facto control, which the authors argue still captures measurement error/biological variability.
- Results are specific to a deload defined as *complete cessation* of training; real-world deloads often involve reduced load/volume/intensity rather than full abstention, and the authors call for future research on those alternative deload styles.

**This note's own interpretation (not stated by authors as a "limitation" but relevant to evidence weighting):**
- Industry funding and a co-author's institutional tie to the funder (Renaissance Periodization, which had a role in study design and manuscript preparation) is a disclosed conflict of interest worth flagging when weighting this evidence, even though the study's methodology (preregistration, blinding, CONSORT reporting) is comparatively rigorous.
- Sample size after dropout (n=18 and n=21) is modest; several credible intervals for strength outcomes were wide, spanning from near-null to fairly large effects, indicating meaningful estimation uncertainty despite posterior probabilities favoring TRAD.
- This is a single trial; no replication currently exists for this specific protocol (complete-cessation, mid-block, 1-week, high-volume hypertrophy program).

## Confidence

**Moderate.** This is a single, well-conducted RCT (randomized, preregistered, blinded outcome assessment, CONSORT-reported, Bayesian analysis with sensitivity checks) — meaningfully stronger evidence than a survey or observational study, and the deload variable was experimentally manipulated (not merely self-reported practice). However, confidence is capped at Moderate rather than High because: it is a single study with no independent replication; sample size is modest (n=39 completers) with wide credible intervals on strength outcomes; the population is narrow (young, university-recruited, previously trained lifters, one specific exercise/machine); only one deload protocol (complete cessation, 1 week, mid-block) was tested, so findings cannot be extrapolated to other deload styles, durations, or timings; and there is a disclosed funding/authorship conflict of interest (Renaissance Periodization funded the study and had a role in study design/manuscript preparation, and a co-author co-founded that company). The hypertrophy null-finding and the strength-detriment finding should each be read as evidence specific to this one protocol, not as generalizable dose-response guidance.
