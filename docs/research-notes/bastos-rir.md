# Citation

Bastos, V., Machado, S., & Teixeira, D. S. (2024). Feasibility and Usefulness of Repetitions-In-Reserve Scales for Selecting Exercise Intensity: A Scoping Review. *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, 131(3), 940–970. https://doi.org/10.1177/00315125241241785

Source PDF:
`08_bastos_rir.pdf.pdf`

## Evidence type

Scoping review (PRISMA-ScR methodology; protocol pre-registered on OSF, registration code JXHY2, registered March 22, 2023). It is a qualitative synthesis of 31 primary experimental/non-experimental studies (N = 855 participants total across all studies) that used a repetitions-in-reserve (RIR) scale. It is **not** a meta-analysis — the authors do not compute pooled effect sizes or a pooled accuracy statistic themselves. They repeatedly reference an external meta-analysis/scoping review (Halperin et al., 2022) for pooled comparisons, but do not reproduce its numeric effect estimates in this paper.

## Research question

PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framing: "Are RIR scales feasible and useful for helping to select/prescribe exercise intensity for resistance training?" Specifically, the review examined (a) how the Estimated Repetitions to Failure (ERF) scale and the RPE-RIR scale have been used in resistance-training (RT) research, (b) how useful they were for prescribing/adjusting exercise intensity, and (c) their contextual feasibility across sex, age, RT experience, exercise type/equipment, and 1-RM intensity.

## Population and evidence base

- 31 included studies, published 2012–2023 (search run through April 30, 2023); all but one (Hackett et al., 2012) published within the last 10 years.
- Total N = 855 participants across all studies (not a single cohort). Sample size per study: <30 participants in 23 studies (74% of studies, 436 participants = 51% of total N); 30–50 in 5 studies (16%, 198 = 23%); 50–100 in 3 studies (10%, 221 = 26%).
- Study design: 3 randomized controlled trials, 2 randomized crossover trials (two of the 31 studies contained two experiments each), and 26 quasi-experimental studies. All studies used convenience sampling.
- Sex: male-only in 16 studies (52%, 307 participants = 36%); both sexes in 14 studies (45%, 534 = 62%); female-only in 1 study (3%, 14 = 2%).
- Age: 18–64 years in 28 studies (90%, 745 participants = 87%); <18 years in 2 studies (7%, 29 = 3%); ≥65 years in 1 study (3%, 82 = 10%).
- RT/training experience: most studies (24, 77%) recruited RT-experienced participants (>6 months regular practice); 4 studies included both experienced and novice groups; 2 studies focused solely on inexperienced individuals; 1 study (older adults) required general exercise experience, not necessarily RT.
- Location: Australia 11 studies (36%, 314 participants = 37%); North America 10 (32%, 321 = 37%); Europe 9 (29%, 202 = 24%); South America 1 (3%, 18 = 2%).
- Only 8 studies (26%) reported an a priori effect-size/power calculation.
- Familiarization with the RIR scale before data collection: the paper gives two different figures for this — Table 1 states 24 studies (78%) were familiarized (5 not familiarized, 16%; 1 partial; 1 unclear), while the Results text states "24 (74%) studies applied prior training and/or familiarization" — this is an inconsistency in the source paper itself, not an error introduced here.
- Scale used: RPE-RIR in 23 studies (74%); ERF in 8 studies (26%).
- Intervention duration varies widely by underlying study, from single laboratory sessions/1-RM tests up to multi-week programs (e.g., 4-week familiarization in Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2018 and Arede et al., 2020; 6-week intervention in Davies et al., 2022; 12-week program in Graham & Cleather, 2021). No single fixed duration applies to the review as a whole.

## Variables investigated

- Accuracy of RIR/RPE-RIR (and ERF) self-estimation versus actual repetitions performed to failure (13 of 31 studies focused directly on this).
- Usefulness of prescribing a target RIR (or RPE-RIR equivalent, e.g., RPE-9 = 1-RIR) to select and adjust training load across sets/sessions (13 of 31 studies).
- RIR used as a study outcome/dependent measure of proximity to failure actually achieved during an intervention, rather than as a prescription tool (5 of 31 studies).
- Moderators of RIR accuracy examined across the included literature: distance from task failure (reps remaining when the estimate is made), sex, RT/training experience, exercise type (single- vs multi-joint), limb region (upper vs lower body), equipment (free weights vs machines), load (%1-RM), and familiarization/repeated-exposure (learning curve).

## Main findings

- **Distance from failure and accuracy**: 10 of 31 research teams (32%) reported that RIR predictions were less accurate the farther the individual was from task failure; the authors state this aligns with the meta-analysis in Halperin et al. (2022) (no pooled correlation value is reproduced in this paper). Individual study examples in Table 2: Ormsbee et al. (2019), Ratto (2019), Remmert et al. (2023), Sinclair et al. (2022), and Sousa (2018) all report predictions farther from failure being less accurate; Zourdos et al. (2016, 2021) similarly found accuracy decreased with more repetitions performed / farther from failure.
- **Load/rep-range effect**: Two studies (Zourdos et al., 2016, 2021) indicated RPE-RIR was less accurate when more repetitions were performed (i.e., at lower relative loads); the authors propose this may reflect higher perceived fatigue/discomfort and metabolic/neuromuscular fatigue accumulation at lower loads with more reps.
- **Sex differences**: Hackett et al. (2017, 2018) found males more accurate than females in estimating repetitions-to-failure with the ERF scale (chest press and leg press exercises). This was not replicated in Remmert et al. (2023), which found no sex differences, and the authors state sex differences "cannot be identified in the meta-analysis of Halperin et al. (2022)."
- **Training experience**: Conflicting evidence. Ormsbee et al. (2019) and Zourdos et al. (2016) suggested RT experience influenced RIR accuracy (more experienced = more accurate); Hackett et al. (2017) and Remmert et al. (2023) found no experience-related differences. Halperin et al. (2022)'s meta-analysis reportedly found no impact of RT experience on RIR accuracy.
- **Exercise/limb differences**: 5 studies (Hackett et al., 2017, 2018; Helms et al., 2017a; Sinclair et al., 2022; Sousa, 2018) reported greater RIR accuracy for upper-body/upper-limb exercises than lower-limb exercises. However, the Halperin et al. (2022) meta-analysis found no significant difference between upper- and lower-body exercises, and in Hackett et al. (2018) the initial-session chest-press-vs-leg-press accuracy difference dissipated by the second session.
- **Learning/repeated-exposure effect**: 5 studies (Hackett, 2022; Hackett et al., 2012, 2018; Mansfield et al., 2020; Sousa, 2018) reported RIR accuracy improved in later sets relative to earlier sets within a session, or across repeated sessions/weeks. Davies et al. (2022) was the only study reporting no accuracy improvement after 6 weeks (attributed by the review authors to a low initial prediction error creating a possible ceiling effect).
- **Usefulness for prescription/adjustment**: In the 13 studies using RIR to prescribe/adjust load, the dominant method was to set a target RIR (e.g., 1-RIR) and adjust load in subsequent sets/sessions if the target was not met. Individual examples: Graham & Cleather (2021) — a 12-week program letting participants self-adjust load to a weekly target RIR produced greater load increases and greater strength gains than a fixed %1-RM prescription group. Shattock & Tee (2022) — an RPE-RIR-based approach improved strength and power, but a velocity-based training approach produced greater improvements than the RPE-RIR approach. Arede et al. (2020) — RIR-based training in youth female athletes was associated with improvements in strength and high-intensity actions (sprinting, jumping, cutting). Bastos et al. (2022) — RPE-RIR used to measure RIR in the first two sets of an exercise and to confirm concentric failure (0-RIR) in the final set.
- **Equipment/exercise-type distribution** (descriptive, not an outcome comparison): free weights only in 23 studies (74%); RT machines only in 6 studies (19%); both in 3 studies (10%). Multi-joint exercises only in 25 studies (81%); both multi- and single-joint in 5 studies; single-joint only in 1 study (Armes et al., 2020, unilateral leg extension).
- **Reliability**: Sinclair et al. (2022) reported "generally acceptable levels of accuracy and moderate-good levels of reliability" for RPE-RIR overall, but "no acceptable levels of reliability and accuracy" specifically for 6-RIR and 9-RIR targets in the bench press, and for 9-RIR in the deadlift.

No pooled correlation coefficients, mean absolute estimation errors (in reps), confidence intervals, or p-values for RIR accuracy are reported by Bastos et al. themselves in the main text extracted; the review reports these as qualitative directional findings per included study (visible in Table 2) rather than as a quantitative synthesis.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This paper is fundamentally about **measurement/prescription feasibility of RIR scales**, not a review of training outcomes (hypertrophy, strength, or endurance) per se. Most of the 31 underlying studies (13 of 31, "accuracy" studies) measured RIR-estimation accuracy in isolation, with no training-outcome endpoint at all.

Where underlying primary studies did report training outcomes, they are heterogeneous and only mentioned briefly in this review, not systematically analyzed for outcome-specific effects:
- **Strength**: Graham & Cleather (2021) reported greater strength gains from RIR-autoregulated load vs. fixed %1-RM prescription over 12 weeks. Shattock & Tee (2022) reported strength and power improvements from an RPE-RIR approach, but smaller than from a velocity-based approach.
- **Power/high-intensity actions**: Arede et al. (2020) reported improvements in sprinting, jumping, and cutting performance in youth female athletes using RIR-based prescription.
- **Hypertrophy**: The review does not report any included study measuring muscle hypertrophy as an outcome. It mentions (in the Discussion, citing other papers, not primary studies in this review) that proximity to failure is generally relevant to hypertrophy and fatigue management, but this is background/introductory context (citing Fisher et al., 2020; Grgic et al., 2022; Izquierdo et al., 2006), not a finding of this review.
- **Endurance**: Not investigated as an outcome anywhere in this review.

In short: this paper does not support or refute RIR's relationship to any specific hypertrophy, strength, or endurance outcome as its primary contribution — its primary contribution is about whether RIR scales can be feasibly and accurately used as a measurement/regulation tool.

## Practical interpretation

The review's qualitative synthesis suggests RIR scales (ERF and RPE-RIR) are broadly feasible and usable, across a wide range of contexts (gym/health club, laboratory, sport-team settings; free weights and machines; ages 18–64), as a subjective, low-cost method for regulating resistance-training intensity. The most consistent methodological pattern across studies was: prescribe a target RIR, have the lifter self-select/adjust load, and adjust load in subsequent sets or sessions based on whether the target RIR was met. Accuracy of the estimate appears to be best when the estimate is made close to muscular failure (low RIR values) and may degrade at higher RIR values / lower relative loads / more repetitions performed. Effects of sex, training experience, and exercise/limb type on accuracy are inconsistent across the individual studies reviewed, and the authors explicitly call for further research to resolve these open questions.

## Application relevance

Highly relevant to an app that logs RIR, but primarily as a **feasibility/measurement-context** paper rather than a source of specific accuracy thresholds or dosing rules.

- **Trackable variable**: Supports RIR as a low-cost, gym-practical variable to log alongside load and reps, consistent with how it was used across the reviewed studies (target RIR set per set/session, load adjusted based on whether target was met).
- **Recommendation signal**: The "prescribe a target RIR, then adjust load in the next set/session if not met" pattern (documented in 13/31 studies) is a *descriptive pattern found in the literature*, not a validated causal rule from this paper — useful as a candidate heuristic seed for an autoregulation feature, but the specific adjustment magnitudes (how much to change load) are not standardized or quantified by this review.
- **Accuracy caveat for automated decisions**: The paper repeatedly documents that RIR self-estimation accuracy (a) declines the farther a set is from failure, (b) may differ by sex, training experience, and exercise/limb type in some (not all) individual studies, and (c) shows a possible early-training "learning curve." This heterogeneity means self-reported RIR should be treated as an imprecise, context-dependent signal rather than a precise input suitable for fully automated micro-adjustments without human judgment or corroborating data.
- **Visualization/default value**: No default RIR targets, thresholds, or "optimal ranges" are prescribed or validated by this review — any defaults an app chooses would need to come from other sources, not this paper.
- **Not suitable for**: automated decisions that assume uniform RIR accuracy across users (novice vs. experienced, male vs. female, upper vs. lower body exercise) — the review shows conflicting evidence on all these moderators.

## What this paper DOES support

- That RIR-based scales (ERF, RPE-RIR) have been used feasibly across a range of RT contexts (gym, lab, team sport) to prescribe and adjust training load, in the specific studies reviewed.
- That RIR estimation accuracy, as reported across several individual studies, tends to be better when the estimate is made closer to task failure (low RIR) than farther from it.
- That some individual studies found accuracy improves with repeated exposure to the scale (a possible learning-curve effect), though this was not tested by every included study and one study found no such improvement.
- That the dominant real-world/study application pattern is prescribing a target RIR and adjusting load to hit it across sets or sessions.
- That some individual studies (not a pooled result) found sex-, experience-, or exercise-type-related differences in RIR accuracy, while others and an external meta-analysis did not replicate these differences — i.e., the paper supports that these moderator effects are unresolved/contested in the literature.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- That RIR can be estimated with a precision sufficient for automated micro-adjustments (e.g., "add weight whenever RIR ≥ 2") — no consistent quantitative error margin (e.g., "± X reps") is established across studies in this review.
- An exact RIR threshold at which weight must be increased or decreased — none is derived or validated by this paper; specific target-RIR values used in underlying studies vary widely by study and population.
- That RIR accuracy is the same across trained and untrained lifters — the review explicitly reports conflicting evidence on this point.
- That RIR accuracy is the same for men and women — conflicting evidence, not resolved.
- That RIR accuracy is the same across upper-body and lower-body / single-joint and multi-joint exercises — conflicting evidence, not resolved.
- Any pooled/meta-analytic effect size, correlation coefficient, or mean estimation error for RIR accuracy calculated by these authors — this paper does not compute one; it is a scoping review, not a meta-analysis.
- Any conclusions about hypertrophy-specific outcomes — no included study measured hypertrophy.
- A validated superiority claim of RIR-based autoregulation over %1-RM or velocity-based methods in general — only isolated individual-study comparisons are cited (e.g., one study favoring RIR autoregulation over fixed %1-RM for strength gains; another finding velocity-based training superior to RPE-RIR).

## Limitations

**Author-stated limitations:**
- RIR scales are relatively new compared to other psychometric scales (e.g., Borg RPE); all but one included study was published within the last 10 years, so the evidence base is recent and still developing.
- Substantial methodological heterogeneity across included studies, particularly in familiarization procedures (timing: prior session vs. during warm-up vs. during pre-test vs. none reported).
- Most studies (23/31, 74%) had small samples (<30 participants) and only 8/31 (26%) reported an a priori power/effect-size calculation.
- Impact of sex, RT experience, and target muscle group (upper vs. lower limb) on RIR accuracy remains unclear/underexplored and is flagged as a priority for future research.
- Underrepresentation of women (52% of studies were male-only samples) leaves a gap in understanding RIR use in women.
- Underrepresentation of older adults and youth (90% of studies sampled 18–64-year-olds).
- Individual conditioning status (e.g., local muscular endurance) is noted as an underexplored factor — the authors cite Hackett & Sabag (2022) as showing greater local muscular endurance may decrease RIR accuracy, and call for more research on this.

**This agent's interpretation (not stated by authors as a formal limitation, but evident from the review's own design):**
- The review does not report having conducted a formal risk-of-bias/methodological quality appraisal of included studies (consistent with, but not mandated against, PRISMA-ScR guidance); readers cannot use this paper to judge the internal validity of individual included studies.
- Because this is a scoping review without pooled quantitative synthesis, claims about accuracy, sex differences, and experience effects are essentially vote-counts across a small number of heterogeneous studies (often only 2–5 studies per specific sub-claim), not statistically pooled or weighted by sample size or study quality.
- All included study designs but 5 (3 RCTs + 2 crossover trials) are quasi-experimental, and all used convenience sampling, which limits causal inference and generalizability.
- The paper itself contains an internal numeric inconsistency (24 studies reported as both 78% in Table 1 and 74% in the Results text for familiarization rate), suggesting some caution is warranted in treating percentages reported in this paper as fully precise.

## Confidence

**Moderate** for the review's core qualitative conclusion (RIR scales are broadly feasible and can be used to prescribe/adjust RT intensity, with better accuracy closer to failure); **Low** for any specific moderator claims (sex, training-experience, or exercise-type effects on RIR accuracy), since these are directly described by the authors as conflicting across the included literature and unresolved even in the cited external meta-analysis. This is a scoping review, not a meta-analysis — it does not itself pool effect sizes, and it does not report a formal quality/risk-of-bias appraisal of included studies. The evidence base is generally recent (mostly <10 years old), consists predominantly of small (74% <30 participants), non-randomized, convenience-sampled studies, with substantial methodological heterogeneity (scale used, familiarization timing, exercise type). "Meta-analysis" status is explicitly not assumed here since this paper is not one.
