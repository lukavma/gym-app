# Citation

Baz-Valle E, Balsalobre-Fernández C, Alix-Fages C, Santos-Concejero J (2022). *A Systematic Review of the Effects of Different Resistance Training Volumes on Muscle Hypertrophy.* Journal of Human Kinetics, 81:199–210. https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2022-0017.

Source PDF:
`docs/research/12_baz_valle_2022_training_volume.pdf`

## Evidence type

Systematic review with meta-analysis (PRISMA-guided), comparing moderate-volume (MV) vs. high-volume (HV) resistance training on muscle hypertrophy, using standardized mean difference (SMD) random-effects models (RevMan 5.4). Study quality assessed via Oxford Levels of Evidence and a modified 8-point PEDro scale.

## Research question

Does a higher weekly set volume per muscle group ("high volume," defined here as >20 weekly sets) produce greater muscle hypertrophy than a "moderate" weekly set volume (12–20 weekly sets) in young, resistance-trained men, and does this dose-response relationship differ by muscle group (quadriceps femoris, biceps brachii, triceps brachii)?

## Population and evidence base

- Literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library (through January 2021), PRISMA-guided; 2,083 records identified → 1,886 after de-duplication → 11 full-text assessed → 7 studies met inclusion criteria (qualitative synthesis) → 6 studies / 14 intervention groups included in the meta-analysis (quantitative synthesis).
- Inclusion criteria: RCTs with explicitly reported set counts, matched load assignment (%1RM or XRM) between compared groups, no external implements (pressure cuffs, hypoxic chambers, etc.), ≥6-week intervention, ≥1 year prior RT experience, participant age 18–35, direct measurement of muscle thickness and/or CSA, published in a peer-reviewed journal.
- Population: young (18–35 y), resistance-**trained** men (≥1 year RT experience) — note one included study (Radaelli et al. 2015) had military/calisthenics-background participants performing bodyweight 5RM bench press, judged by the review authors to meet the experience bar.
- Studies grouped by weekly-set volume per muscle group into three descriptive bands: low volume (LV, <12 sets/week — included qualitatively only, excluded from the quantitative MV-vs-HV meta-analysis), moderate volume (MV, 12–20 sets/week), high volume (HV, >20 sets/week).
- Study durations: 6–24 weeks (five of seven studies 6–8 weeks; one 24 weeks [Radaelli 2015]).
- Methodological quality: modified PEDro scores averaged 4.7±1.1 (range 3–6, out of 7 max on the modified scale); 4 of 7 studies rated Oxford level 1b (high-quality RCT), 3 rated 2b.
- Outcome measured via direct ultrasound (muscle thickness) in all included studies; no indirect/circumference-based measures used.

## Variables investigated

Weekly resistance-training set volume per muscle group (categorized moderate 12–20 vs. high >20 sets/week; low <12 sets/week qualitative-only), analyzed **separately by muscle group**: quadriceps femoris (vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, and/or anterior-thigh composite measurements — inconsistently defined across studies), biceps brachii, and triceps brachii. All single- and multi-joint (indirect/synergist) sets contributing to a given muscle's volume were counted, per Schoenfeld et al.'s (2019b) set-counting proposal (i.e., full-credit counting of both direct and indirect sets — a different counting convention than Pelland et al.'s fractional/0.5-credit approach elsewhere in this corpus).

## Main findings

- **Quadriceps femoris (quantitative, 8 effects/93 MV vs. 88 HV participants across studies):** No significant difference between MV and HV, P=0.19. SMD = −0.20 [95% CI −0.49, 0.10], favoring HV numerically but not significant. I²=0% (homogeneous). Qualitatively, 2 of 7 individual measurements found significant MV<HV differences (both favoring high volume); the remaining showed no significant difference either direction. Improvement percentages ranged 1.6–13.7% across MV groups and were numerically larger in HV in 4 of 5 studies reporting percentages (13.3%, 9.4%, 12.5%, 13.7% HV vs. 4.9%, 6.9%, 7.5% MV in the other three) — but this qualitative percentage comparison is descriptive, not the same as the pooled significance test.
- **Biceps brachii (quantitative, 6 effects/70 MV vs. 73 HV):** No significant difference, P=0.59. SMD = −0.10 [95% CI −0.46, 0.26], favoring HV numerically but not significant. I²=14% (still low heterogeneity). Qualitatively, 2 of 5 studies found significant differences favoring HV (one HV>LV, one HV>MV and HV>LV).
- **Triceps brachii (quantitative, 5 effects/55 MV vs. HV):** Significant difference favoring HV, P=0.01. SMD = −0.50 [95% CI −0.88, −0.11], moderate effect size favoring high volume. I²=0% (homogeneous). This is the review's one clear muscle-specific dose-response finding: 3 of 4 qualitative studies measuring triceps found a favorable trend toward high volume.
- **Muscle-specific asymmetry, not general dose-response:** the review explicitly found NO significant MV-vs-HV difference for quadriceps or biceps, but DID find a significant, moderate-magnitude difference for triceps — i.e., "higher volume is better" did not hold uniformly across muscles tested.
- **Authors' proposed explanation for the triceps exception:** the triceps brachii acts predominantly as a synergist (not prime mover) in many multi-joint pressing exercises; because indirect/synergist sets were counted toward triceps volume under the full-credit convention used, the authors speculate more total volume (built up partly via multi-joint work) may be needed to reach an equivalent direct training stimulus compared to a muscle more consistently trained as a prime mover.
- **Author-proposed overall recommendation (their own conclusion, not itself a further statistical result):** "a range of 12-20 weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation for increasing muscle hypertrophy in young, trained men" for quadriceps and biceps specifically, with triceps as an explicit exception where >20 sets (or, when isolating just direct triceps-focused work, possibly as few as 12–20 *direct* sets once multi-joint synergist volume is also counted) may be more effective.
- **No low-volume (<12 sets) quantitative comparison exists:** the meta-analysis could not compare low vs. moderate/high volume because too few included studies had a true <12-sets/week arm — LV data are qualitative/descriptive only, drawn from a small number of studies, several of which found low volume clearly inferior (e.g., Radaelli 2015: 1.9% LV vs. 7.6% MV vs. 17.5% HV bicep gains, described as statistically significant HV>MV>LV>control in that single study).

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This paper examines **muscle hypertrophy only** (via direct ultrasound thickness/CSA measurement). It makes no claims about strength, power, or endurance outcomes — strength was not a study inclusion criterion or outcome measure.

## Practical interpretation

For quadriceps and biceps specifically, this review's pooled, homogeneous (I²=0%) data found no statistically detectable hypertrophy advantage of training beyond ~20 weekly sets compared to the 12–20 set range, in young trained men over 6–24 weeks. For triceps specifically, the opposite pattern held — a moderate, statistically significant, homogeneous advantage for >20 sets. The authors interpret this asymmetry as plausibly related to the triceps' more consistent role as a secondary/synergist muscle in the exercise pool used across the underlying studies, meaning a given "set" of direct triceps work may need to be supplemented by more indirect volume to produce an equivalent total stimulus — but this is the authors' own proposed explanation, not something independently tested by a mechanistic experiment in this review. The review's own recommendation language ("12-20 weekly sets... may be an optimum standard recommendation") should be read as the authors' synthesis judgment resting on 6 studies (quads/biceps) or 4 studies (triceps), not as a precisely established, individually-calibrated optimum.

## Application relevance

This paper is directly relevant to `docs/evidence/research-gaps.md` GAP-01 (per-muscle-group volume landmarks). It should be evaluated separately against four distinct possible product claims, per the following breakdown:

- **(A) Tracking volume per muscle group is a meaningful, evidence-motivated practice:** Supported. The entire premise of this review — that hypertrophy responses can differ meaningfully by muscle group at the same categorical volume level (triceps vs. quadriceps/biceps) — is direct justification for tracking and reasoning about volume per muscle group rather than only as an aggregate/whole-body number.
- **(B) Using population-level reference ranges (e.g., "12–20 sets/week is a reasonable general range for many muscles") as a default, editable, non-authoritative starting point:** Weakly-to-moderately supported for quadriceps and biceps specifically, in the trained young-male population studied, over 6–24 weeks. This is defensible as a *default/heuristic* range, not as a scientifically established optimum.
- **(C) Exact muscle-specific optimal set targets (e.g., "quadriceps: exactly 16 sets is optimal"):** NOT supported. The review compares two broad categorical bands (12–20 vs. >20 sets); it does not identify a specific optimal number within or across those bands, and explicitly could not test volumes below 12 sets/week quantitatively at all.
- **(D) Exact muscle-specific MRV (maximum recoverable volume) values:** NOT supported. This review has no true upper bound in its data — "high volume" (>20 sets) is an open-ended, unbounded category (the actual HV group set-counts in the underlying studies ranged from 24 to 45 sets/week; see Table 2), and no muscle group showed a point at which additional volume became clearly *worse* (as an MRV concept would require) — only "no better" (quads/biceps) or "better" (triceps). This paper cannot be used to support or calibrate the Renaissance Periodization MRV construct or any other specific maximum-recoverable-volume number.
- Do NOT claim this paper validates RP's specific MV/MEV/MAV/MRV numeric landmarks for any muscle group — RP's framework uses four distinct, individually-defined landmarks (minimum, maintenance/entry, adaptive maximum, and maximum recoverable), while this review tests only a two-category (moderate vs. high) comparison with an open-ended high-volume ceiling and no low-volume quantitative arm. The concepts are related in spirit (both propose volume "zones") but this paper does not supply numeric validation for RP's specific thresholds.

## What this paper DOES support

- No significant hypertrophy advantage of >20 vs. 12–20 weekly sets for quadriceps femoris (SMD −0.20, P=0.19) or biceps brachii (SMD −0.10, P=0.59) in young trained men.
- A significant, moderate hypertrophy advantage of >20 vs. 12–20 weekly sets specifically for triceps brachii (SMD −0.50, P=0.01).
- Muscle-group-specific volume dose-response can differ — volume recommendations should not necessarily be assumed uniform across all muscle groups.
- A broad, defensible default range (12–20 weekly sets) for quadriceps/biceps hypertrophy programming in trained young men, framed as a heuristic starting point.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- Any claim about strength, power, or endurance outcomes.
- A quantitative comparison of low (<12 sets/week) volume against moderate or high volume — this was descriptive/qualitative only, due to too few qualifying low-volume study arms.
- Precise, individually-calibrated optimal set counts for any muscle group.
- Any maximum recoverable volume (MRV) ceiling for any muscle — the high-volume category is open-ended (>20 sets, up to 45 sets/week in the underlying data) with no observed point of diminishing or negative returns.
- Generalization to women (all included studies were male-only or reported male-only subgroups for the relevant outcomes), to untrained or minimally-trained individuals (all participants had ≥1 year RT experience), to individuals outside 18–35 years old, or beyond quadriceps/biceps/triceps to other muscle groups.
- Validation of any specific commercial/coaching volume-landmark framework's exact numeric thresholds (e.g., RP's MV/MEV/MAV/MRV values).

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
1. Small number of included studies overall (7 qualitative, 6/14-arm quantitative) — a narrow evidence base, especially per muscle group (5 studies for triceps, 6 for biceps, 8 effects for quads).
2. No standardization of what counts as "moderate" or "high" volume across the underlying primary studies — bands were imposed post hoc by the reviewers (12–20 vs. >20), making direct comparison across studies somewhat approximate.
3. Quadriceps femoris measurements were inconsistent across studies (vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, anterior thigh distal/proximal/total composite) — comparing "quadriceps hypertrophy" pools measurements of different specific sites.
4. Calorie intake and supplementation were not reported/controlled in most of the analyzed studies, a potentially important hypertrophy confound.
5. Difficulty comparing low-volume groups to moderate/high groups due to a lack of qualifying <10–12 weekly-set study arms.

**Agent-assessed (not stated by authors):**
- The "high volume" category has no upper bound in this review's design — HV group set-counts across the underlying studies ranged roughly 24–45 sets/week (Table 2), meaning the significant triceps finding and the null quad/biceps findings are being driven by a heterogeneous mix of "moderately high" and "very high" volumes lumped into one category; the review cannot say whether, within the >20 band, there is a point of diminishing or negative returns.
- Full-credit (not fractional) counting of indirect/synergist sets is used here (per Schoenfeld et al. 2019b), which differs from the fractional (0.5-credit) approach found to best-fit the dose-response data in Pelland et al. 2024 elsewhere in this corpus (`pelland-volume-frequency.md`, EVIDENCE-004) — the two papers' volume numbers are not directly comparable without accounting for this different counting convention, and this may partly explain why triceps (a common synergist muscle) shows an unusual pattern here.

## Confidence

**Low-Moderate.** This is a peer-reviewed, PRISMA-guided systematic review with meta-analysis, homogeneous pooled estimates (I²=0–14% across all three muscle groups, indicating consistent effects across the included studies), and a deliberately narrow/restrictive inclusion criteria set intended to improve sample homogeneity relative to prior broader reviews (e.g., Schoenfeld 2017, Krieger 2010). However, confidence is capped at Low-Moderate because: the underlying evidence base is very small per muscle group (5–8 effects), modest average methodological quality (PEDro 4.7/7), no true low-volume quantitative arm exists, the high-volume category is unbounded and heterogeneous internally, and one of the three muscle-specific findings (triceps) rests on only 4-5 studies with a somewhat speculative post hoc mechanistic explanation (synergist-muscle involvement) that was not itself directly tested.

## Cross-reference note

This paper should be read alongside `pelland-volume-frequency.md` (EVIDENCE-001) as a complementary but methodologically distinct volume source: Pelland provides a **pooled, cross-muscle-group, continuous** dose-response curve (fractional set-counting, weekly sets 0–~40+, β=0.24%/set) across a much larger evidence base (67 studies), while Baz-Valle provides a **muscle-specific, categorical** (moderate vs. high) comparison across a much smaller evidence base (6-7 studies) using full-credit set counting. Neither paper individually resolves GAP-01 (exact per-muscle volume landmarks); together they support (a) volume matters for hypertrophy in general (Pelland), and (b) the exact volume-response relationship may not be uniform across all muscle groups (Baz-Valle's triceps exception) — but neither supplies the precision needed for exact per-muscle-group prescriptive targets.
