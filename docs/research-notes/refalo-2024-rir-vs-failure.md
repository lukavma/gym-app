# Citation

Refalo MC, Helms ER, Robinson ZP, Hamilton DL, Fyfe JJ (2024). *Similar muscle hypertrophy following eight weeks of resistance training to momentary muscular failure or with repetitions-in-reserve in resistance-trained individuals.* Journal of Sports Sciences, 42(1):85–101. https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021.

Source PDF:
`docs/research/13_refalo_2024_rir_vs_failure.pdf`

**Disambiguation note:** This is a different paper from `05_refalo_proximity_failure.pdf` (Refalo et al. 2023, systematic review + meta-analysis — see `refalo-proximity-to-failure.md`) and from `14_refalo_2024_rir_accuracy.pdf` (Refalo et al. 2024, RIR-accuracy JSCR study — see `refalo-2024-rir-accuracy.md`). All three share overlapping authorship (Refalo, Helms, and/or Fyfe/Hamilton) but are three distinct publications. This note covers the **8-week longitudinal within-participant RCT** comparing training to failure vs. training with RIR targets.

## Evidence type

Randomized controlled trial — within-participant (unilateral/contralateral-limb randomization), single intervention arm structure (each participant served as their own control across limbs), 8-week duration, peer-reviewed (Journal of Sports Sciences).

## Research question

In resistance-trained individuals, does an 8-week lower-body resistance training program performed to momentary muscular failure (FAIL) produce different quadriceps hypertrophy, strength, or neuromuscular fatigue outcomes compared to an equivalent program performed with a prescribed repetitions-in-reserve target (RIR; 1–2 RIR depending on exercise) that stops short of failure?

## Population and evidence base

- 18 participants in the primary analysis (12 males, 6 females; originally 19 enrolled, 1 female excluded for nutritional non-adherence — a sensitivity analysis including the full n=19 in supplementary material confirmed the primary results were robust to this exclusion).
- Resistance-**trained**: males 26.9±3.1 y, 7.8±2.6 y RT experience; females 30.0±5.8 y, 7.5±2.3 y RT experience. Authors state this is among the highest reported training experience of any study comparing failure vs. non-failure training to date.
- Within-participant, contralateral-limb design: each participant's two legs were independently randomized to FAIL or RIR, removing between-subject confounds (genetics, nutrition, sleep, adherence) from the primary comparison.
- Two exercises trained unilaterally, 2×/week, ~72 h apart, 16 sessions total over 8 weeks (≥14/16 = 90% session completion required for inclusion): leg press (8–10RM range) and leg extension (10–12RM range).
- Set volume individualized per participant from habitual quadriceps training volume at baseline (males 12±1 sets/week, females 14±2 sets/week), increased 20% at week 5 (progressive volume increase built into the design, not held constant throughout).

## Variables investigated

- **FAIL protocol:** every set on both exercises taken to momentary muscular failure (0-RIR, unable to complete another repetition with proper form).
- **RIR protocol:** sets terminated at a prescribed RIR target that differed by exercise — 2-RIR on leg press, 1-RIR on leg extension (not a single uniform RIR value across exercises).
- Primary outcome: quadriceps muscle thickness (ultrasound, average of rectus femoris [RF] and vastus lateralis [VL], single site each).
- Secondary/mechanistic outcomes: leg press and leg extension 1RM-equivalent strength; within-session neuromuscular fatigue (lifting velocity loss from first to final set); within-session repetition loss (first-to-final-set rep count decline); accumulated volume load and repetition volume across the 8 weeks.
- Analysis: Bayesian linear mixed-effects models reporting posterior mean, highest-density credible intervals (HDI), probability of direction (pd), and probability the effect exceeds a smallest-effect-size-of-interest / typical-error threshold (pd>TE).

## Main findings

- **Quadriceps thickness (primary hypertrophy outcome):** FAIL +0.181 cm [HDI 0.119, 0.243], pd=100%, +6.96%; RIR +0.182 cm [HDI 0.115, 0.247], pd=100%, +6.98%. Both protocols produced clear, credible hypertrophy. Between-protocol difference: −0.001 cm [HDI −0.063, 0.058], pd=48% (essentially a coin-flip for direction), pd>TE=3% (only 3% probability the difference exceeds a typical-error-sized effect) — i.e., **no meaningful difference** between FAIL and RIR (1–2 RIR) for overall quadriceps hypertrophy.
- **Muscle-specific divergence:** rectus femoris thickness gain was numerically greater under RIR (+7.38%) than FAIL (+5.98%), 81% probability of direction (pd>TE=42%); vastus lateralis gain was numerically greater under FAIL (+7.95%) than RIR (+6.59%), 79% probability of direction (pd>TE=22%). Neither muscle-specific difference reached the paper's threshold for a clearly credible effect (pd>TE well under 50% for both), and the authors attribute the pattern to exercise-order/fatigue-carryover effects (leg press performed before leg extension within each session) rather than to proximity-to-failure itself.
- **Neuromuscular fatigue (velocity loss, first-to-final set):** FAIL consistently showed greater within-session velocity loss than RIR across the intervention — Week 1: FAIL −9.9% [HDI −14.8, −5.0] vs. RIR −4.4% [HDI −9.1, 0.7], pd=98%; Week 4: FAIL −12.6% [−18.0, −7.2] vs. RIR −5.8% [−11.1, −0.5], pd=99%; Week 8: FAIL −9.6% [−15.1, −3.7] vs. RIR −6.4% [−12.0, 0.6], pd=99%. The between-protocol gap narrowed somewhat from Week 1 (pd=98%) to Week 8 (pd=85%), suggesting some fatigue-resistance adaptation over time, though the pattern varied by exercise.
- **Repetition loss (first-to-final set):** Leg press FAIL −20.4% [HDI −27.0, −13.9] vs. RIR −15.8% [−22.8, −9.3], pd=100%. Leg extension FAIL −29.9% [−33.8, −25.9] vs. RIR −21.4% [−25.8, −17.6], pd=100%. FAIL produced consistently greater within-session repetition decline than RIR.
- **Accumulated volume load and repetition volume:** not deliberately equalized between protocols (allowed to vary naturally as a consequence of each protocol's own stopping rule). Despite this, similar mean volume load and repetition volume accumulated across the 8 weeks between FAIL and RIR, with similar longitudinal trends (Figure 6). The authors interpret this as: the high within-session repetition loss under FAIL (fatigue accumulating faster) offset the theoretical "each set closer to failure = greater per-set stimulus" advantage, such that total accumulated training volume ended up comparable between protocols anyway.
- **Strength outcomes:** reported for leg press and leg extension; the paper's central strength emphasis in the abstract/key points is on hypertrophy rather than strength divergence — no clearly credible between-protocol strength difference is highlighted as a key finding (consistent with the general failure-vs-non-failure literature pattern already captured in this corpus for strength).

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

Primary outcome and main finding are about **hypertrophy** (quadriceps thickness): FAIL and RIR (1–2 RIR) produced statistically indistinguishable, similarly large hypertrophy. Strength outcomes were also measured but are not the paper's headline finding and are not reported here with the same effect-size specificity extracted for hypertrophy; no strong between-protocol strength divergence is emphasized by the authors. This paper should not be treated as primary strength-outcome evidence in this corpus — its hypertrophy findings are its main contribution.

## Practical interpretation

In resistance-trained individuals with quadriceps-focused unilateral training over 8 weeks, stopping sets 1–2 reps short of failure produced hypertrophy statistically indistinguishable from training to failure, while producing consistently less within-session neuromuscular fatigue and less within-session repetition loss. This supports the general proposition that momentary muscular failure is not clearly required to produce substantial hypertrophy — training reasonably close to failure (1–2 RIR, in this specific protocol) can be sufficient. This should be stated cautiously: the RIR protocol used two different targets (1-RIR and 2-RIR) across the two exercises, not a single uniform RIR value, so this paper is evidence for "close-to-failure training in the 1–2 RIR neighborhood," not for pinpointing a single optimal RIR number. The muscle-specific divergence (RF favoring RIR, VL favoring FAIL) further cautions against treating "RIR ≈ failure" as a clean, uniform, muscle-independent conclusion — the authors themselves attribute this divergence to exercise order effects, not to proximity-to-failure, but it demonstrates the finding is not perfectly homogeneous across muscles.

## Application relevance

This paper is directly relevant to `docs/evidence/research-gaps.md` GAP-02 (proximity-to-failure curve shape) and to the existing Robinson-vs-Refalo(2023) disagreement documented in `docs/evidence/evidence-registry.md` §5. It adds **intervention-trial** evidence (as opposed to Robinson's and the 2023 Refalo meta-analysis's cross-study/meta-regression evidence) directly comparing failure to a specific close-to-failure RIR target, in a well-controlled within-participant design. It should be read as supporting the general claim "training within roughly 1–2 reps of failure can be sufficient for hypertrophy; literal failure does not appear necessary" — this is a claim about a *range*, not a precise optimal RIR value. It does **not** establish an exact optimal RIR (the two exercises used different targets, 1 vs. 2, precisely because there wasn't a single tested value), and it does not resolve the full shape of the proximity-to-failure dose-response curve (e.g., how 1–2 RIR compares to 4–5 RIR or higher). This paper also usefully illustrates why "sets to failure" and "high stimulus per set" are not automatically the same as "more accumulated volume" — because repetition loss under FAIL can offset its per-set advantage over an 8-week block, an important nuance if this application ever reasons about the relationship between proximity-to-failure prescriptions and expected accumulated volume.

## What this paper DOES support

- Training within 1–2 RIR (i.e., stopping reasonably close to, but short of, momentary muscular failure) can produce hypertrophy statistically indistinguishable from training to failure, in resistance-trained individuals, over 8 weeks, for the quadriceps.
- Momentary muscular failure is not clearly necessary to produce substantial, credible hypertrophy.
- Training to failure produces greater acute within-session neuromuscular fatigue (velocity loss) and greater within-session repetition loss than training with 1–2 RIR, consistently across an 8-week block.
- Total accumulated training volume (volume load, repetition volume) can end up similar between a to-failure and a 1–2 RIR protocol even without deliberately equating volume, because higher repetition loss under failure training tends to offset its per-set proximity advantage.
- The proximity-to-failure effect on hypertrophy is not necessarily perfectly uniform across muscles within the same limb/session (RF vs. VL divergence), plausibly moderated by exercise order/fatigue carryover.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- Do NOT infer that 1-RIR or 2-RIR specifically is "the optimal RIR" — the study did not test a single RIR value in isolation, used different targets per exercise, and did not compare a range of RIR values against each other (only against 0-RIR/failure).
- Does NOT establish the full proximity-to-failure dose-response curve shape (e.g., no comparison to 3-RIR, 4-RIR, or higher).
- Does NOT provide strong, headline strength-outcome evidence — hypertrophy is the paper's primary and best-supported finding.
- Does NOT generalize beyond the quadriceps (specifically RF and VL, single ultrasound site each) — no other muscle groups were measured, so extrapolation to upper body or other lower-body muscles is unsupported by this paper directly.
- Does NOT generalize confidently beyond resistance-trained populations with ~7–8 years average training experience, nor beyond an 8-week timeframe.
- Does NOT support a specific automated trigger rule (e.g., "if RIR ≥ 2, do X") — it supports the general viability of RIR-target-based (non-failure) training as an alternative to failure training, at the architecture/strategy level, not a specific decision threshold.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
1. Small sample size (n=18 primary, n=19 sensitivity).
2. Only one muscle-group family assessed (quadriceps: RF + VL only), single ultrasound measurement site per muscle, so regional hypertrophy within the quadriceps cannot be resolved.
3. Only 8 weeks — longer-term outcomes unknown.
4. RIR protocol used different targets per exercise (1-RIR leg extension, 2-RIR leg press) rather than one uniform RIR value, complicating interpretation of "the RIR condition" as a single well-defined intervention.
5. Whether findings generalize to other exercises or muscle groups is unclear.
6. Sex was included in the statistical models but not specifically analyzed as a moderator of the failure-vs-RIR effect.

**Agent-assessed (not stated by authors):**
- The within-participant contralateral design is a strength for removing between-subject confounds, but cross-education/systemic training effects between limbs (well documented in the strength-training literature) remain a theoretical concern for any unilateral-limb RCT design, potentially attenuating true between-protocol differences somewhat (this is a general design caveat, not specific evidence the authors report testing).
- The practical-application graphical abstract (Figure 7) proposing that general-population trainees should stay ~2-RIR while bodybuilders/hypertrophy-focused individuals should train closer to failure is the authors' own interpretive/practical extrapolation, explicitly framed by them as illustrative examples — it is not itself a further statistical finding from this study and should not be cited as if it were a tested result.

## Confidence

**Moderate-High** for the core finding that 1–2 RIR training and failure training produce statistically indistinguishable quadriceps hypertrophy over 8 weeks in trained individuals (well-controlled within-participant design, credible-interval-based Bayesian analysis with pd=48%/pd>TE=3% clearly indicating no meaningful difference, consistent with the directionally similar finding already in this corpus from the 2023 Refalo meta-analysis). **Low-Moderate** for the more specific sub-claims (muscle-specific RF/VL divergence, accumulated-volume-offset mechanism) given the smaller strata, single-study nature, and partly-speculative mechanistic explanations offered by the authors themselves.

## Cross-reference note

This paper's Table 3 also reports RIR prediction accuracy data (absolute error <1 rep for both 1-RIR and 3-RIR targets, slight overestimation trend closer to failure and underestimation trend further from failure) as a secondary/incidental measurement within this longitudinal trial. This is a smaller, less rigorously isolated accuracy analysis than the dedicated `refalo-2024-rir-accuracy.md` (paper 14) study, but is directionally consistent with it and with `bastos-rir.md` (EVIDENCE-014) on the general pattern that RIR accuracy tends to be better closer to failure. This paper should be read alongside `refalo-proximity-to-failure.md` (2023 meta-analysis, EVIDENCE-011/012) as complementary: the 2023 paper pools cross-study meta-analytic evidence on failure vs. non-failure broadly, while this 2024 paper contributes a single, well-controlled, within-participant intervention directly comparing failure to a specific close-to-failure (1–2 RIR) target — together strengthening (not merely repeating) the claim that literal failure is not required for hypertrophy, while still leaving the exact dose-response curve shape unresolved (GAP-02).
