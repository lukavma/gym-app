## Evidence type

Position stand / consensus statement built on an **overview of reviews (umbrella review)** of randomized controlled trials — i.e., a systematic review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses, not a new pooled meta-analysis of primary data. Methodological quality of each included review was rated with AMSTAR (1–11 scale); outcome-level certainty was scored with a GRADE-informed "quality of evidence" (QoE) percentage the authors developed for this synthesis (no standardized method exists for grading certainty in overviews of reviews, per the authors). The paper updates the ACSM 2009 Position Stand, "Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults."

## Research question

What is the impact of resistance training (RT) prescription (RTx) variables — frequency, load, failure/effort, time under tension, time of day, inter-set rest, exercise order, training type (e.g., blood-flow restriction, free-weight vs. machine, unstable surface, variable load), contraction type, technique (Olympic-style weightlifting, range of motion, power training), volume (sets), set structure, and periodization — on muscle strength, hypertrophy, power, muscular endurance, contraction velocity, and physical function (balance, gait speed, chair-stand, timed up-and-go, stair climbing, etc.) in healthy adults, compared with no exercise or an alternative RT prescription?

## Population and evidence base

- Population: healthy adults ≥18 yr, "no defined disease(s)," explicitly including obesity, sarcopenia, and physical frailty as still counting as "healthy." Reviews were not stratified by age; both under- and over-55-yr populations were pooled, so the authors state recommendations are evidence-based "across all ages" but not age-specific.
- Training status: most included reviews studied novice/inexperienced trainees; some included more advanced trainees. The authors note training experience previously showed minimal impact on strength/hypertrophy outcomes (cited from their own prior work) but this was not independently re-analyzed here.
- Sex: not reported as a stratifying variable in this synthesis.
- Evidence base: 137 systematic reviews (of RCTs) included, from 5,751 records after de-duplication, 480 assessed at full text (PRISMA flow, Fig. 1). Combined, the included reviews represent >30,000 participants. AMSTAR scores of included reviews ranged 1–9 out of a possible 11 (wide quality spread).
- Intervention duration: RT programs ≥6 wk (range 6–52 wk), minimum 12 exposures, vs. a no-exercise control or a distinct RT prescription (differing on ≥1 FITT-VP component).
- Overlap of primary studies across reviews (double-counting risk) was quantified only for the strength outcome using the corrected covered area (CCA) index, showing "moderate" overlap (6–10% of primary papers appearing in ≥2 reviews). Overlap was not formally assessed for other outcomes.
- Number of underlying primary RCTs is not given as a single figure; participant counts (n) reported per variable in Tables 3–5 are outcome/variable-specific and drawn from "the most common measurement in reviews reporting outcome-specific sample sizes."

## Variables investigated

Frequency (sessions/wk); Load (%1RM/RM); Failure (training to volitional failure vs. not); Time under tension; Time of day; Inter-set rest; Exercise order; Type (blood-flow restriction, free-weight, machine, unstable surface, variable load); Contraction type (eccentric overload vs. traditional); Technique (Olympic-style weightlifting, partial vs. full range of motion, power/velocity-based training); Volume (number of sets); Set structure (drop sets, cluster, complex, contrast, rest-redistribution, traditional); Periodization (periodized/block/linear vs. nonperiodized/undulating). Outcomes: strength (1RM), hypertrophy (muscle size), power, muscular endurance, contraction velocity, and physical function tests (gait speed, balance, chair-stand, timed up-and-go, stair climbing, multicomponent function, SPPB, walking, running, jumping, change-of-direction/agility, functional reach).

## Main findings

The paper does **not** report pooled effect sizes (SMD/CI/p-values) itself; its "results" are review-count tallies (↑ = positively impacts, ✗ = does not impact, ? = insufficient/indeterminate data), participant totals, and the custom QoE% per variable (Tables 3–6). Selected figures, preserved as given:

- **RT vs. no-exercise control (Table 3):** Standard RT improved strength (↑26 reviews, n=23,204, QoE=73%; 3 reviews indeterminate; 1 review ↓, n=488, QoE=50%), hypertrophy (↑12 reviews, n=14,924, QoE=79%), and power (↑4 reviews, n=1,001, QoE=63%). Circuit RT (↑3 reviews, n=843, QoE=92% for strength; ↑1 review, n=236, QoE=100% for hypertrophy) and elastic-band RT (↑2 reviews, n=1,921, QoE=63% for strength) also improved outcomes vs. control. Data were insufficient (ND) to determine effects of eccentric flywheel, Nordic hamstring, Olympic-style weightlifting, or unstable-surface RT on hypertrophy or power vs. control.
- **Between distinct RTx (Table 4), strength:** positively affected by training frequency (↑4 reviews, n=3,531, QoE=69%), load (dose-response; ↑6 reviews, n=6,574, QoE=79%), eccentric flywheel devices (↑1 review, n=332, QoE=75%), full range of motion (↑2 reviews, n=1,262, QoE=50%), volume/sets (↑7 reviews, n=5,633, QoE=71%), and exercise order (↑4 reviews, n=941, QoE=88%). Not affected by: training to failure, machine vs. free-weight, unstable vs. stable surfaces, time under tension, time of day, inter-set rest duration, or contraction type.
- **Between distinct RTx, hypertrophy:** positively affected by contraction type (eccentric overload; ↑1 review, n=868, QoE=75%) and volume/sets (dose-response; ↑5 reviews, n=2,267, QoE=50%). Not affected (or evidence insufficient) for frequency, load (when volume was equated, load 30% vs. 100% 1RM did not differ), fatigue/failure, blood-flow restriction, variable loading, time under tension, power training, periodization, or exercise order.
- **Between distinct RTx, power:** positively affected by moderate loads (30%–70% 1RM; ↑2 reviews, n=1,980, QoE=79%), eccentric flywheel devices (↑1 review, n=235, QoE=75%), and power-training technique (↑2 reviews, n=863, QoE=75%); not affected by set structure.
- **Physical function (Table 5), RT vs. control:** gait speed (↑8 reviews, n=3,407, QoE=81%), balance (↑1 review, n=406, QoE=75%), chair-stand (↑2 reviews, n=954, QoE=88%), timed up-and-go (↑5 reviews, n=1,568, QoE=90%) all improved with standard RT. Stair climbing was indeterminate (ND). SPPB was not improved by standard RT vs. control (1 review), which the authors attribute to sparse evidence rather than a true null, since SPPB's individual components (gait speed, balance, chair-stand) each improved.
- **Dose-response detail (Discussion, citing a separate meta-regression by Swinton et al.):** weekly-sets analysis showed progression with volume but a "dose-response that plateaued and showed diminishing returns beyond ~2–3 sets/exercise for strength and ~18–20 weekly sets for hypertrophy," across varying loads. One set was superior to zero (control), and two sets superior to one, but "the exact number of sets required to optimize adaptations cannot be ascertained."
- **Safety:** in an analysis of >38,000 participants (>6,700 in RT arms, >11,000 older adults), RT did not increase risk of serious adverse events; nonserious AE risk was not different from aerobic exercise. In 23 studies of coronary heart disease patients (n=1,174), all 63 nonfatal cardiovascular complications occurred during aerobic training (none during RT); 20 musculoskeletal complications occurred during RT and resolved with intensity/position changes.
- **Participation context (background, not an RTx finding):** ~30% of US adults self-report meeting muscle-strengthening guidelines (≥2 d/wk); ~60% report none. Older-adult participation estimates range ~1%–40% across UK/US/Australia data.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

- **Strength** is most consistently enhanced by: higher relative load (≥~80% 1RM, dose-response), training frequency ≥2 sessions/wk, ≥2–3 sets per exercise, full range of motion, eccentric flywheel devices, and performing key lifts at the beginning of a session. Strength was **not** measurably affected by training to failure, machine vs. free-weight equipment, unstable vs. stable surfaces, contraction speed, time of day, or inter-set rest length in the reviewed data.
- **Hypertrophy** is most consistently enhanced by eccentric contractions/overload and higher training volume (≥~10 sets/muscle group/wk, dose-response). Notably, when volume was equated, hypertrophy was **not** differentiated by load (30% vs. 100% 1RM) or by frequency (1 d/wk vs. >5 d/wk) — i.e., volume, not load or frequency per se, was the variable most tied to hypertrophy in this synthesis. Training to failure, blood-flow restriction, periodization, and exercise order showed no or insufficient effect on hypertrophy.
- **Power** responded to moderate loads (30%–70% 1RM), eccentric flywheel devices, Olympic-style weightlifting, and power-training (fast-concentric) technique — a distinct set of levers from strength or hypertrophy. The authors explicitly caution that jumping performance should not be treated as a proxy for muscle power.
- **Muscular endurance and physical function** outcomes improved with RT generally (vs. control), but there was insufficient evidence to attribute these improvements to any specific RTx variable (load, volume, set structure) — this evidence should not be merged with the strength/hypertrophy/power findings above.

## Practical interpretation

A cautious practitioner could reasonably conclude that essentially any structured RT program (standard, circuit, elastic-band, home-based) outperforms no exercise for strength, hypertrophy, power, and most functional outcomes in healthy adults, and that RT is safe across ages including in cardiac populations. Beyond "do some RT with genuine effort ≥2×/week," the variable-specific guidance is directional rather than prescriptive: higher relative load and adequate volume/frequency favor strength; adequate volume (with genuine eccentric loading) favors hypertrophy more than load or frequency choice; moderate loads and ballistic/eccentric-overload techniques favor power. The paper itself frames individualization — not adherence to a fixed FITT-VP recipe — as the primary practical takeaway, explicitly moving away from the prescriptive 2009 Position Stand defaults.

## Application relevance

- Trackable variables directly supported by this evidence base: sessions/week (frequency), sets per exercise/per week (volume), relative load (%1RM), and exercise order — all have at least some dose-response or comparative signal.
- Reasonable heuristic seeds (not hard rules): minimum of ≥2 sets/exercise and ≥2 sessions/wk as a floor for strength benefit; framing weekly set volume as a trackable/visualizable trend is defensible since the paper notes this "weekly volume load" metric was rarely even computed in the underlying literature — an app could visualize it as an exploratory metric, not an evidence-validated target.
- Effort/proximity-to-failure (RIR) is flagged by the paper as important conceptually but **not quantifiable** from current evidence — not suitable for encoding a specific RIR threshold as a default or automated rule.
- Not suitable for automated decision-making: an "optimal" universal number of sets, a specific RIR/percent-effort cutoff, periodization scheme selection (periodization was not shown superior to non-periodized, volume-equated programs), or ranking of one RT modality against another (this overview cannot support comparative-effectiveness claims; the authors say network meta-analysis would be required for that).

## What this paper DOES support

- RT of many forms (standard, circuit, elastic-band, home-based) improves strength, hypertrophy, power, and several physical-function outcomes compared with no exercise, in healthy adults of varying ages.
- Higher relative load and adequate weekly volume (sets) are each associated, in a dose-response manner, with greater strength and hypertrophy gains, respectively, up to a point beyond which the included meta-regression evidence shows diminishing returns.
- Training frequency of at least 2 sessions/week is associated with strength benefit; the upper limit of beneficial frequency could not be determined.
- RT is safe for healthy adults across the age spectrum, including in people with coronary heart disease, with no elevated risk of serious adverse events relative to comparators.
- Progressive overload is not strictly required for beneficial (vs. no-exercise) outcomes to occur, though the authors hypothesize it matters for continued long-term progress.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- A single universal "optimal" number of sets, reps, or sessions — the authors explicitly state the exact number of sets needed cannot be ascertained; only a plateauing dose-response trend is described.
- A universal RIR / percent-effort / proximity-to-failure numeric threshold — evidence was judged insufficient to quantify exact RIR or perceived-exertion targets.
- A specific optimal periodization scheme or mesocycle length — periodization (as a category) was not shown superior to nonperiodized training under volume-equated conditions, and specific block/linear/undulating structures were not resolved individually.
- Head-to-head superiority rankings between RT modalities (e.g., "free weights are better than machines," "unstable surfaces are better/worse") beyond the narrow single-review comparisons reported, most of which were themselves indeterminate or based on very small evidence.
- Age-specific, sex-specific, or trained-vs-untrained-specific prescriptions — the underlying reviews were not stratified this way in the current synthesis.
- Precise blood-flow-restriction (BFR) load/pressure prescriptions — BFR effects are confounded with load in the underlying literature and cannot be isolated.
- Any claims about clinical/diseased populations generally (only cardiac-safety data specifically) or about minors — population is healthy adults ≥18 yr.
- RP-style volume-landmark terminology (MV/MEV/MAV/MRV) — this framework is not used or evidenced anywhere in this paper.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- RCTs in sports/exercise medicine are frequently poor quality (small samples, weak randomization, lack of preregistration, incomplete adverse-event reporting).
- No standardized method exists for grading certainty of evidence in overviews of reviews; a fully cohesive GRADE evaluation across the whole synthesis was not possible, so a custom QoE% metric was used instead.
- Overlap/double-counting of primary studies across included reviews was only formally quantified for the strength outcome (moderate overlap, 6–10%); other outcomes carry unassessed overlap risk.
- An overview of reviews cannot support comparative-effectiveness (head-to-head) conclusions between RT interventions; this would require network meta-analysis.
- Blood-flow-restriction and eccentric-flywheel study designs confound their key variable (BFR, eccentric overload) with load/contraction type, so individual effects cannot be isolated from available data.
- Muscular endurance testing methodology (absolute vs. relative; pre- vs. post-intervention basis) was inconsistent across studies and not accounted for in this synthesis.
- No sensitivity analyses were conducted; outcome data were tabulated as collected.

**My own interpretation (not stated by authors):**
- The headline ✓/✗/? symbols in Tables 3–6 are a coarse ternary vote-count of review-level bottom-line judgments, not pooled effect sizes; two "✓" ratings can represent very different strength of evidence (e.g., 26 reviews/n=23,204 vs. 1 review/n=44 both appear as "✓" or "?" with no visual weighting for the reader).
- Because the paper reports no SMDs, CIs, or p-values of its own, any effect-size figures a downstream reader might want (e.g., "how much stronger") must come from the individual meta-analyses cited within, not from this overview.
- Risk of bias at the primary-RCT level is once-removed: this overview trusts AMSTAR ratings of the included reviews rather than directly re-appraising the underlying trials for each outcome.

## Confidence

**Moderate**, with a caveat that certainty is uneven across claim types. Confidence is higher for top-line claims (RT vs. no-exercise improves strength/hypertrophy/power/most function outcomes) because they draw on large numbers of independent reviews and >30,000 total participants with directionally consistent results. Confidence is lower for granular RTx-variable-level and dose-response claims, because many individual variable rows in Tables 4–6 rest on just 1–4 reviews with QoE scores frequently in the 25%–75% range, review overlap was not corrected outside the strength outcome, the synthesis method is a narrative vote-count rather than a formal pooled meta-analysis (no SMDs/CIs reported), and included review quality (AMSTAR) ranged as low as 1/11. All comparisons originate from RCTs (experimentally manipulated RTx variables), which is a strength, but heterogeneity in populations, outcome definitions, and RTx operationalization across the 137 source reviews was not statistically quantified at the overview level.
