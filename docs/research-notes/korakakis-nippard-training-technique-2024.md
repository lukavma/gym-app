"Optimizing Resistance Training Technique to Maximize Muscle Hypertrophy: A Narrative Review." *Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology* 2024, 9(1), 9. https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk9010009. Received 29 Nov 2023; accepted 25 Dec 2023; published 29 Dec 2023.

## Evidence type

Narrative review (journal-labeled "Review," not "Systematic Review"). No methods section, systematic search strategy, PRISMA flow, or formal quality/risk-of-bias appraisal is present. The paper synthesizes a mix of existing systematic reviews/meta-analyses and individual primary studies selected narratively by the authors, rather than through a documented search protocol.

## Research question

What constitutes proper resistance-training (RT) exercise technique for maximizing muscle hypertrophy, with focus on: repetition tempo/contraction type, range of motion (ROM), and exercise-specific kinematics (body positioning/movement patterns, including "strict" vs. "non-strict"/momentum-assisted technique). Aim: synthesize the evidence into technique recommendations and identify future-research needs (p. 1–2).

## Population and evidence base

No single pooled population; this is a narrative synthesis of separate primary studies and reviews, each with its own sample. No aggregate participant count or total study count is given. Populations named for individual cited studies include: sedentary individuals (Keeler et al., tempo study), older men (Nogueira et al., tempo study), trained men (Pereira et al., biceps eccentric-tempo study; Pearson et al., leg-extension eccentric-tempo study), university soccer players (Shibata et al., squat eccentric-tempo study), women (Gillies et al., tempo/fiber-type study). Intervention durations are only stated for isolated studies (e.g., Nunes et al. foot-position calf study = 9 weeks); no aggregate duration range is reported. Training-status detail for the ROM-specific studies (Wolf et al. meta-analysis, Kassiano et al. reviews/study) is not specified in the text extracted. Not reported: overall number of studies synthesized, overall N, sex/age ranges as a whole.

## Variables investigated

- Repetition tempo / duration of eccentric and concentric phases (contraction type)
- Range of motion: full ROM vs. partial ROM, and specifically partial ROM performed at long vs. short muscle lengths
- Exercise-specific kinematics: body positioning, joint angles, foot/grip placement
- "Strict" vs. "non-strict" (lenient) technique — i.e., degree of ancillary-muscle/momentum involvement during a target-muscle exercise

## Main findings

**Tempo**
- Schoenfeld et al. (cited systematic review/meta-analysis): repetition durations from 0.5–8 s produced similar hypertrophy; did not test specific eccentric/concentric combinations (p. 2).
- Wilk et al. (cited review): slower eccentric + faster concentric tempo "seems" best for hypertrophy, but heterogeneity precluded a specific per-phase recommendation; suggested ~3–8 s/rep overall (p. 2).
- Keeler et al.: eccentric 5 vs. 4 s, concentric 10 vs. 2 s — no significant pre-to-post lean body mass (LBM, via Bod Pod) change in either group; strength/aerobic-capacity changes occurred "in the absence of changes in LBM" (p. 2–3).
- Nogueira et al. (older men): faster concentric (1 s) produced significantly greater biceps brachii and rectus femoris hypertrophy than slower concentric (3 s) (p. 3).
- Pereira et al.: 4 s eccentric produced greater absolute biceps brachii hypertrophy than 1 s eccentric, but the difference was **not statistically significant**; effect sizes favored the longer-eccentric group (p. 3).
- Unnamed study [18]: 2 vs. 4 s eccentric — overall lower-limb hypertrophy similar between groups, but the 4 s eccentric group had significantly greater vastus medialis thickness increases (p. 3).
- Pearson et al. (trained men, leg extension): 1 s eccentric produced marginally greater quadriceps thickness gains than 3 s eccentric (p. 3).
- Shibata et al. (parallel squat): 2 s vs. 4 s eccentric — no difference in thigh cross-sectional area (p. 3).
- Gillies et al.: 6 s concentric/2 s eccentric group showed significantly greater vastus lateralis type I **and** type IIa fiber-area increases; the 6 s eccentric/2 s concentric group showed increases only in type I fiber area (p. 3).
- Authors' synthesis: "significant increases in hypertrophy can occur with repetition durations between 2 and 8 s" — a broad, flexible window; unclear whether biasing eccentric or concentric duration confers extra benefit (p. 3, Table 1).

**Range of motion**
- Older dichotomized (full vs. partial) systematic reviews/meta-analyses found a hypertrophic benefit of full ROM over partial ROM but did not account for muscle length (p. 3).
- Wolf et al. (cited meta-analysis): a subgroup analysis found partial ROM at long muscle lengths potentially superior to full ROM for hypertrophy — but based on only **3 studies**; caution urged (p. 3–4).
- Kassiano et al. (cited systematic review): partial ROM at long muscle lengths is sufficient for optimal growth of quadriceps femoris, biceps brachii, and triceps brachii (p. 3).
- Kassiano et al. (new primary study, ankle plantarflexion): 3 arms — full ROM, shorter-muscle-length partial ROM (50%), longer-muscle-length partial ROM (50%). Medial gastrocnemius grew significantly more with longer-length partial ROM than with either full ROM or shorter-length partial ROM. Lateral gastrocnemius grew significantly more with **both** full ROM and longer-length partial ROM vs. shorter-length partial ROM (no significant full-vs-long-partial difference for this head) — a head-specific dissociation within the same muscle (p. 4).
- Unpublished conference abstract (methods not published — interpret cautiously): full ROM vs. longer-length partial ROM (50%) on a multi-hip machine; hip-extensor composite hypertrophy, gluteus maximus, and long head of biceps femoris were significantly greater with longer-length partial ROM; semitendinosus/semimembranosus differences were not significant, though effect sizes favored the partial-ROM group (p. 4).
- Authors' synthesis: current literature supports biasing ROM toward long muscle lengths as the default for hypertrophy; whether shortening the range at the short-length end reduces benefit is unclear, and stopping short of full ROM may even enhance hypertrophy via a different mechanism (cited: sustained intramuscular hypoxia, ref. 29) (p. 4, Table 1).

**Exercise-specific kinematics**
- Nunes et al. (9-week study): different foot positions during calf training produced greater hypertrophy of different calf-muscle regions (portion-specific effect) — one of the few direct studies on kinematic manipulation and hypertrophy (p. 5).
- Other cited studies show foot position/grip width affect EMG activation in leg press, lat pulldown, bench press, but the authors note activation is not a validated predictor of hypertrophy, so practical relevance is "questionable" (p. 5).
- Injury-related technique guidance (e.g., avoiding lumbar flexion) is often based on cadaver/biomechanical modeling rather than longitudinal or cross-sectional evidence in living humans (p. 5).

**Strict vs. non-strict technique**
- Only one indirect study identified: Arandjelovic — a mechanical-modeling/simulation study (lateral raise), not an empirical hypertrophy trial. Moderate momentum at rep onset could permit heavier loads ("better overload") in biomechanically advantageous positions; excessive momentum reduces target-muscle loading and could impair stimulus. Findings "must be viewed with circumspection" (p. 5).
- Authors state: "there is no literature directly examining the effect of strict versus non-strict repetition techniques on hypertrophy" (p. 5–6).

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This entire review is scoped to **hypertrophy** outcomes only (title, abstract, and Table 1 recommendations are explicitly framed for muscle growth). It does not derive or endorse ROM/tempo/technique recommendations for maximal strength, power, or endurance. The one point of direct strength/hypertrophy dissociation reported: in Keeler et al., significant improvements in strength and aerobic capacity occurred "in the absence of changes in LBM" — i.e., strength gains did not track lean-mass (hypertrophy) changes under that tempo protocol (p. 2–3). Wilk et al.'s cited review covered both strength and hypertrophy tempo adaptations, but this paper draws only the hypertrophy-relevant conclusion from it. Muscle/region-specific dissociations reported (all hypertrophy outcomes, not strength): biceps brachii and rectus femoris responded differently to concentric tempo in older men (Nogueira et al.); vastus medialis vs. overall lower-limb hypertrophy diverged by eccentric duration (unnamed study [18]); vastus lateralis type I vs. type IIa fiber area diverged by which phase (concentric vs. eccentric) was lengthened (Gillies et al.); medial vs. lateral gastrocnemius heads responded differently to ROM condition (Kassiano et al.); semitendinosus/semimembranosus vs. gluteus maximus/biceps femoris long head responded differently to ROM condition in the unpublished hip-extensor abstract.

## Practical interpretation

The authors' own practical recommendations (Table 1, p. 6):
- **Tempo** (evidence rated "Moderate" by the authors): a total repetition duration of 2–8 s appears sufficient to maximize hypertrophy; unclear whether biasing eccentric or concentric phase duration adds further benefit.
- **ROM** (evidence rated "Moderate"): employ a ROM that allows the target muscle to be fully stretched (i.e., bias toward long muscle lengths); traversing the short end of ROM may not add benefit.
- **Non-target-muscle involvement** (evidence rated "N/A" — no direct evidence exists): minimize use of external momentum/ancillary-muscle assistance where possible, as a precautionary default rather than an evidence-based rule.
- Exercise-specific body-positioning/kinematic guidelines are recommended to continue following existing (largely biomechanics-derived, not hypertrophy-tested) conventions, because direct research is too limited to revise them (abstract, p. 6).

## Application relevance

- ROM (bias toward long muscle length within an exercise) and total repetition tempo (2–8 s window) are potentially trackable/loggable technique variables, but the paper supports only a broad qualitative heuristic ("favor long muscle lengths," "stay within a wide tempo band"), not a specific numeric ROM percentage or tempo prescription.
- The "N/A" evidence rating for ancillary-muscle involvement / strict-vs-lenient technique means this variable is **not suitable** for an automated recommendation rule — the review explicitly states no direct hypertrophy evidence exists on it.
- Exercise-specific kinematic checklists (grip width, stance, bar path) should not be encoded as hypertrophy-optimizing rules from this paper; the authors say these are based on biomechanics/anatomy convention and safety, not hypertrophy research, and should simply be retained as-is pending future study.

## What this paper DOES support

- That a broad range of repetition tempos (roughly 2–8 s total per rep) can support significant hypertrophy — tempo is more flexible than sometimes assumed.
- That, based on current (limited) evidence, biasing ROM toward long muscle lengths is a reasonable default for maximizing hypertrophy, and that partial ROM emphasizing long muscle lengths can match or exceed full ROM for some muscles (quadriceps femoris, biceps brachii, triceps brachii per Kassiano et al.; gastrocnemius heads and hip extensors per newer/unpublished studies).
- That ROM effects can differ by specific muscle and even by muscle head/region within the same muscle (e.g., medial vs. lateral gastrocnemius).
- That exercise-specific body-positioning guidelines are currently based on anatomical/biomechanical reasoning and injury-prevention logic, not on hypertrophy-outcome research.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- That full ROM is always superior for every muscle or exercise.
- An exact, universally optimal ROM percentage or muscle-length threshold (the "50%" partials cited are specific protocol parameters from individual studies, not a general prescription).
- A specific optimal eccentric or concentric phase duration, or that manipulating eccentric vs. concentric duration reliably changes hypertrophy outcomes (findings across cited studies conflict).
- Any conclusion about strict vs. non-strict (momentum-assisted) technique and hypertrophy — the authors state directly that no such literature exists.
- Any hypertrophy-specific evidence base for most exercise-specific kinematic guidelines (grip width, stance, foot angle) beyond the single calf foot-position study and a few EMG-only studies of uncertain relevance.
- Any strength, power, or endurance-specific technique recommendations — the paper's scope and Table 1 recommendations are hypertrophy-only.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- Research directly examining exercise technique's effect on hypertrophy (as opposed to safety/performance) is "scarce" (p. 2).
- The Wolf et al. ROM subgroup finding (partial ROM at long length superior) rests on only 3 studies; authors explicitly urge caution (p. 3–4).
- The multi-hip-machine ROM study is an unpublished conference abstract with unpublished methods; results "must be interpreted cautiously" (p. 4).
- The Arandjelovic strict/non-strict paper is a mechanical simulation, not an empirical measure of muscle mass change over time; "findings...must be viewed with circumspection" (p. 5).
- EMG/muscle-activation studies on kinematics have questionable practical relevance because activation is not a validated predictor of hypertrophy (p. 5, citing refs 34–35).
- Injury-avoidance kinematic guidance (e.g., lumbar flexion) is often derived from cadaver/mechanistic studies rather than living-human longitudinal/cross-sectional evidence (p. 5).
- Long-muscle-length ROM literature is currently limited to a few muscles; unclear whether some muscles are more predisposed to benefit from long-length training than others (p. 4).
- Authors explicitly call for future research on strict vs. lenient technique and on stricter vs. more lenient kinematic variation generally (p. 6–7, Conclusions).

**Reviewer-added observations (not stated by the authors):**
- No systematic search methodology, PRISMA process, or risk-of-bias/quality appraisal is described anywhere in the text, which limits confidence that all relevant evidence was captured or weighted appropriately — this is inherent to the narrative-review format and was not disclosed as a limitation by the authors.
- The review draws on a small number of individual primary studies per topic (often single studies per specific comparison, e.g., one study each for concentric-speed effects in older men, one for calf foot-position), so many "findings" summarized above are single-study results, not replicated effects.
- Conflicts of interest disclosed by the paper itself (not flagged as a "limitation" by the authors but relevant to interpretation): B.J. Schoenfeld serves on the scientific advisory board of Tonal Corporation (fitness equipment manufacturer); J. Nippard was employed by STRCNG Incorporated (Jeff Nippard Fitness) (p. 7).

## Confidence

**Moderate-Low.** This is a narrative review with no disclosed systematic search strategy or quality appraisal, so completeness and selection of cited evidence cannot be independently verified. Many individual conclusions rest on single small primary studies (some in narrow populations like older men or soccer players) with directly conflicting results across studies (e.g., eccentric-duration effects on hypertrophy vary by muscle and study). The most-cited ROM finding (partial ROM at long muscle length ≥ full ROM) is explicitly caveated by the authors themselves as resting on very few studies (3-study subgroup; one unpublished abstract). The authors do rate their own tempo and ROM conclusions as only "Moderate" evidence quality, and rate the non-target-muscle-involvement recommendation as "N/A" (no evidence). Given the review synthesizes real experimental hypertrophy trials (not just theory) but has these systematic/quality gaps, "Moderate-Low" (between the template's Moderate and Low) is the most accurate rating; if forced to pick a single template category, choose **Low** for any claim beyond the tempo range and long-muscle-length-ROM-bias headline conclusions, and **Moderate** for those two headline conclusions specifically.
