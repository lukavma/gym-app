# Citation

Chaves TS, Scarpelli MC, Bergamasco JGA, da Silva DG, Medalha Junior RA, Dias NF, Bittencourt D, Carello Filho PC, Angleri V, Nóbrega SR, Roberts MD, Ugrinowitsch C, Libardi CA (2024). *Effects of Resistance Training Overload Progression Protocols on Strength and Muscle Mass.* International Journal of Sports Medicine, 45:504–510. https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2256-5857. Accepted 2024-01-29, published online 2024-03-12.

Source PDF:
`docs/research/11_chaves_2024_overload_progression.pdf`

## Evidence type

Randomized controlled trial — prospective, single-blind (assessors blinded), intrasubject (within-subject) design with each leg randomized to a different protocol. CONSORT-reported, CONSORT extension for within-subject designs used, prospectively registered (retrospectively logged in a clinical trials registry).

## Research question

Does progressing resistance-training overload by increasing **load** (LOADprog: load raised as the individual moves outside the prescribed rep-max zone) produce different strength and muscle cross-sectional-area (CSA) outcomes than progressing overload by increasing **repetitions** at a fixed relative load (REPSprog: load fixed at 80% 1RM throughout, reps allowed to increase set to set until concentric failure)? Secondary aims: compare volume-load (VL) progression between protocols, and test whether VL progression correlates with strength/hypertrophy gains within each protocol.

## Population and evidence base

- 39 previously untrained young adults (20 men, 19 women), 18–35 years old (age 24±4 y, body mass 68.2±12.4 kg, height 1.70±0.08 m, BMI 23.5±3.7 kg/m², relative knee-extension strength 1.53±0.34 kg/bm), no RT or other structured physical training in the prior ≥6 months, no musculoskeletal injury or neuromuscular disorder of the lower limbs.
- Single-center, single exercise (unilateral leg extension), 10-week intervention, 2–3 sessions/week (23 total training sessions completed by all analyzed participants; weekly frequency varied but did not differ systematically between protocols since both legs of the same person trained on the same days).
- Within-subject design: each participant's two legs were randomized (stratified block randomization by leg dominance and by strength-asymmetry tertile between legs) to LOADprog on one leg and REPSprog on the other — this design removes between-subject confounds (nutrition, sleep, training history, genetics) from the load-vs-reps comparison.
- 141 people screened → 53 randomized → 39 (78 legs) included in final analysis (CONSORT flow: 10 dropped before starting; 3 discontinued for personal reasons; 1 withdrew consent before final assessment).
- Sample size was power-calculated a priori (Superpower/RStudio simulation, 10,000 iterations): minimum n=38 for 80% power, α=0.05, r=0.72 inter-leg correlation, f=0.12 (Cohen's f) for the ordinal protocol×time interaction.

## Variables investigated

Two progressive-overload strategies, both trained to concentric muscular failure every set: **LOADprog** (4 sets, 9–12RM zone, 90 s rest; load increased when the individual exceeded 12 reps) vs. **REPSprog** (4 sets, load fixed at 80% 1RM for the entire program; load only increased once the individual could complete more reps than the previous session, i.e., reps allowed to climb indefinitely each set until muscle failure). Outcomes: 1RM (unilateral leg-extension machine test) and vastus lateralis CSA (panoramic B-mode ultrasound). Secondary/process measures: accumulated repetitions, accumulated volume load (VL = sets × reps × load), relative/absolute load at first vs. last session, and the within-protocol correlation between VL progression and 1RM/CSA gains.

## Main findings

- **1RM (primary outcome):** both protocols increased 1RM significantly from pre to post (main effect of time, F=213.6, P<0.0001). No significant protocol×time interaction (F=1.71, P=0.20) and no significant protocol main effect (F=3.21, P=0.08). LOADprog: 52.9±16.3 → 69.0±18.5 kg (Δ=16.1±7.2 kg, +32.8%). REPSprog: 51.7±15.8 → 66.8±17.9 kg (Δ=15.1±7.0 kg, +31.4%). Mean between-protocol difference = −1.0 kg [95% CI −2.6 to 0.6], numerically favoring LOADprog but not statistically significant.
- **Muscle CSA (primary outcome):** both protocols increased CSA significantly (main effect of time, F=94.07, P<0.0001). No significant protocol×time interaction (F=0.02, P=0.87) and no significant protocol main effect (F=0.57, P=0.45). LOADprog: 21.3±4.7 → 23.5±5.4 cm² (Δ=2.2±2.2 cm², +11.2%). REPSprog: 21.1±4.6 → 23.4±5.2 cm² (Δ=2.3±1.7 cm², +10.5%). Mean between-protocol difference = 0.1 cm² [95% CI −0.5 to 0.8], numerically favoring REPSprog but not statistically significant.
- **Accumulated repetitions over the program:** significantly higher for REPSprog than LOADprog (LOADprog: 1000±67 reps; REPSprog: 1292±302 reps; mean difference [95% CI] −292 reps [−375 to −207], T=−7.041, P<0.0001) — mechanically expected, since REPSprog's overload mechanism is doing more reps at a fixed load.
- **Accumulated load lifted:** significantly higher for LOADprog than REPSprog (LOADprog: 1282±392 kg total load progression; REPSprog: 989±307 kg; mean difference 293 kg [212 to 372], T=7.384, P<0.0001) — again mechanically expected.
- **Relative/absolute load, first vs. last session:** significant protocol×time interaction for both absolute (F=27.66, P<0.0001) and relative load (F=101.87, P<0.0001). LOADprog absolute load rose from 42.3±13.1 kg (pre, ~80%1RM) to 61.4±20.7 kg (post, ~88%1RM), Δ=19.0±12.9 kg, P<0.0001. REPSprog absolute load stayed flat (41.6±12.7 kg pre → 41.6±12.7 kg post, Δ=0±0, by protocol design) while relative load dropped from 80%1RM to 62%1RM (P<0.0001), since 1RM rose but the fixed 80%-of-baseline-1RM load did not.
- **Accumulated volume load (VL):** NOT significantly different between protocols (LOADprog: 53,703±17,390 kg; REPSprog: 52,528±18,283 kg; mean difference [95% CI] 1,174 kg [−1,289 to 3,637]; T=0.96, P=0.346). Area-under-the-curve analysis of VL progression across the 23 sessions also showed no significant between-protocol difference (T=1.32, P=0.19).
- **Correlation between VL progression and outcome gains:** no significant correlation for either protocol, for either outcome — LOADprog/strength: R=0.20 [95% CI −0.11, 0.50], P=0.20; REPSprog/strength: R=0.16 [−0.16, 0.46], P=0.31; LOADprog/CSA: R=−0.04 [−0.35, 0.28], P=0.80; REPSprog/CSA: R=0.08 [−0.24, 0.38], P=0.64.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This paper directly tests **both** outcomes in the same design and finds **no meaningful divergence between them**: both LOADprog and REPSprog produced statistically indistinguishable strength gains AND statistically indistinguishable hypertrophy gains. This is different from several other papers in this corpus (e.g., Lopez on load, Moesgaard on periodization) where strength and hypertrophy findings diverge from each other — here, the two progression *strategies* converge on both outcomes instead.

## Practical interpretation

In previously untrained young adults training a single-joint lower-body exercise to concentric failure every set for 10 weeks, **how** overload is progressed (raising load within a rep-max zone vs. raising reps at a fixed relative load) did not produce a detectably different strength or hypertrophy outcome. Both protocols necessarily converged on similar accumulated volume load despite using opposite progression mechanics (LOADprog did less total reps but lifted more total kg; REPSprog did the reverse) — the authors suggest this convergence in VL, achieved via two different routes, is the likely reason outcomes were similar, since both groups trained with a wide range of loads and reps but always to muscular failure. The authors explicitly frame this as evidence that **either progression protocol is usable** for early-stage untrained trainees, not that the two are proven interchangeable in all contexts (e.g., trained lifters, other exercises, longer durations, or when not trained to failure were not tested).

## Application relevance

- This is the paper the existing evidence registry (§6, pre-papers-11–14) explicitly flagged as absent: "No paper in this corpus directly and systematically studies progression-scheme dose-response." Paper 11 fills that specific gap with a controlled, within-subject RCT directly comparing load progression vs. repetition progression.
- Directly supports an architecture-level (not algorithm-level) conclusion: **the application should be able to represent and support more than one progression strategy** (e.g., a `progressionStrategy` field/interchangeable strategy pattern), since neither load-progression nor repetition-progression is shown superior — both are evidence-consistent choices.
- Does **not** support deriving a specific automated trigger rule (e.g., "if RIR ≥ 2, add 2.5 kg") — this study does not test RIR-based triggers at all; both protocols used a fixed structural rule (load raised outside a rep zone; reps raised at a fixed load) rather than an RIR-based decision, and both were taken to full concentric failure every set, which is itself a specific (high-effort) condition not universally assumed by RIR-based approaches.
- Supports treating volume load (sets × reps × load) as a plausible common currency across differing progression schemes, since VL accumulation converged despite very different weekly rep/load trajectories — though the paper itself found no significant correlation between VL progression and either outcome, so VL should not be treated as a validated predictive metric either.

## What this paper DOES support

- Both load progression and repetition progression, applied to failure every set, produced significant, similar-magnitude strength gains in untrained adults over 10 weeks.
- Both produced significant, similar-magnitude hypertrophy (CSA) gains over the same period.
- No statistically significant between-protocol difference was found for either outcome (strength or hypertrophy).
- Accumulated volume load did not differ meaningfully between the two progression mechanisms despite their very different session-to-session load/rep trajectories.
- The application architecture should be able to support multiple overload-progression strategies rather than assuming one (e.g., load-only progression) is the sole valid form of overload.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- A claim that load progression and repetition progression are proven equivalent in **all** populations, exercises, or durations — this is one exercise (leg extension), one duration (10 weeks), one population (untrained young adults), with both protocols trained to concentric failure.
- Any specific automated trigger rule for deciding when to progress (e.g., specific RIR thresholds, specific %1RM increments) — the study used simple structural rules (rep-zone boundary or feasible-reps-at-fixed-load), not an RIR-based autoregulation trigger, and does not test RIR-based triggers at all.
- A claim about which protocol is superior in trained/advanced lifters — population is untrained-only.
- Generalization beyond a single-joint exercise or beyond lower-body/knee-extensor musculature.
- A claim that volume load is a validated causal driver of outcomes — no significant VL-outcome correlation was found within either protocol.
- Any claim about non-failure training, since both protocols were trained to concentric muscular failure every set — this paper does not speak to proximity-to-failure as a variable.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- Only the vastus lateralis (single muscle) was assessed; non-uniform hypertrophy can occur within a single muscle and only one measurement point was used.
- Muscle strength and RT were limited to the lower limb (unilateral leg extension) and should not be extrapolated to other exercises or muscle groups.
- Sample was exclusively previously untrained individuals — cannot ascertain whether different adaptive behaviors would appear in trained/resistance-experienced individuals or other age groups.
- Only 10 weeks of training — conclusions cannot be assumed to hold over a longer training period.
- Lack of significant correlation between VL progression and outcomes does not imply the absence of any relationship — the authors note the true relationship could be more complex than a simple linear correlation.

**Agent-assessed (not stated by authors):**
- Because both protocols were driven to concentric muscular failure on every set, this study isolates "how overload is applied" from "how close to failure training is taken" — it cannot be used as evidence about non-failure progression schemes, RIR-based autoregulated progression, or double progression (a third common progression scheme in practice, e.g., increase reps within a range, then increase load) which was not tested here at all.
- The within-subject (same-person, contralateral-leg) design is a methodological strength for isolating the progression-scheme variable from between-subject confounds, but cross-education/systemic training effects between limbs (a known phenomenon in unilateral RT research) are not discussed by the authors and could theoretically attenuate true between-protocol differences, though this is a general limitation of unilateral within-subject RT designs rather than one specific to this study.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** This is a single peer-reviewed RCT (not a meta-analysis or systematic review), so it lacks independent replication for this specific comparison — but its within-subject design is a notable methodological strength (removes between-subject variability, a major source of noise in RT research), it was adequately powered a priori for the tested interaction, used blinded outcome assessment, and reported complete CONSORT-compliant flow and effect estimates with confidence intervals for all key comparisons. The clean, non-significant interaction terms for both primary outcomes (with reasonably tight CIs, e.g., 1RM MD −1.0 kg [−2.6, 0.6]) support a genuine null finding rather than an underpowered non-result. Confidence is capped below High because it is a single study, restricted to untrained participants and one exercise, with only a 10-week duration.

## Cross-reference note

This paper is the corpus's first and only direct, controlled, within-subject test of a progression-*scheme* comparison (load vs. repetitions) and should be read alongside `bastos-rir.md` (EVIDENCE-015), which only describes a *descriptive pattern* of RIR-based autoregulation use in the literature without a controlled comparison. Chaves does not test RIR-based autoregulation at all — it tests two structural (non-RIR) progression rules, both driven to failure. The two notes are complementary (one is a controlled experiment on load-vs-reps progression, the other is a descriptive/observational pattern on RIR-based load adjustment) but should not be merged into a single claim, since they address different questions.
