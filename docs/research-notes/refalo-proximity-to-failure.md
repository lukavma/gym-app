## Evidence type

Systematic review with meta-analysis (PRISMA-guided), including multi-level random-effects meta-analysis, sub-group analyses, moderator checks, and sensitivity analyses.

## Research question

What is the influence of resistance training (RT) proximity-to-failure on skeletal muscle hypertrophy? Specifically: (1) What is the overall effect of RT performed to set failure (any definition) versus non-failure, and do the specific definition of set failure applied, volume load, or relative load moderate this? (2) Does the magnitude of velocity loss achieved (and theoretically, proximity-to-failure) during RT influence hypertrophy? (3) What magnitudes of hypertrophy are achieved when RT is performed to momentary muscular failure, to set failure (other definitions), and to a high velocity loss?

## Population and evidence base

- 15 studies included, drawn from PubMed, SCOPUS, and SPORTDiscus searches (to September 2021) plus 2 studies added manually, following the authors' prior scoping review's thematic framework.
- Studies grouped into three themes by set-termination definition: **Theme A** — momentary muscular failure vs. non-failure (5 studies); **Theme B** — other set-failure definitions vs. non-failure (4 studies); **Theme C** — different velocity-loss (VL) thresholds, no true failure group (6 studies).
- Population: apparently healthy adults of any age and RT experience (mix of untrained [UT] and trained [T] participants per study; Table 2). Sex: mostly male-only studies; one female-only (n=89); a few mixed-sex cohorts.
- Individual study sample sizes range from n=10 to n=89 (Table 2). No single pooled total N is stated in the text; summing the 15 individual study samples gives approximately 540 participants, though two of the Theme C studies (Pareja-Blanco et al. 2020, n=64 each) may involve related/overlapping cohort recruitment — this is not clarified in the text.
- Ages range roughly from young adult (~20 years) to older adult (65.4 ± 4.5 years in one Theme B study).
- Intervention durations: 6–14 weeks, 2–3 sessions/week.
- Methodological quality (TESTEX scale, max 15): scores ranged 7–12, mean/median 9.9/10; 11 of 15 studies scored >10 ("high quality").
- All 6 Theme C (velocity-loss) studies were conducted in previously **trained** participants only.

## Variables investigated

Proximity-to-failure, operationalized **categorically** (not as a continuous RIR estimate): momentary muscular failure vs. non-failure; other set-failure definitions vs. non-failure; and velocity-loss threshold (low <20%, moderate 20–25%, high >25%, i.e., 40% or 50% VL in the included studies). Outcome: muscle hypertrophy only (muscle thickness, whole-limb/muscle cross-sectional area or volume, or lean mass via DXA/BIA). Moderators tested: volume-load equated vs. not; relative load (>50% 1RM vs. ≤50% 1RM); velocity-loss magnitude.

## Main findings

- **Overall (Theme A+B combined), set failure vs. non-failure:** trivial advantage for set failure. ES = 0.19 (95% CI 0.00, 0.37), p = 0.045. Heterogeneity: Q = 6.65, df = 17, p = 0.988, I² = 0% (18 effects across 9 studies, low heterogeneity).
- **Theme A (momentary muscular failure vs. non-failure):** trivial, non-significant. ES = 0.12 (95% CI −0.13, 0.37), p = 0.343. Q = 4.58, df = 8, p = 0.801, I² = 7.38% (5 studies).
- **Theme B (other set-failure definitions vs. non-failure):** trivial, non-significant. ES = 0.27 (95% CI −0.03, 0.57), p = 0.077. Q = 1.60, df = 8, p = 0.991, I² = 0% (4 studies).
- **Sub-group by volume load** (across Theme A+B): equated ES = 0.20 (95% CI −0.03, 0.43), p = 0.09; not equated ES = 0.17 (95% CI −0.13, 0.47), p = 0.27. Moderator effect not significant (p = 0.884).
- **Sub-group by relative load:** higher load (>50% 1RM) ES = 0.15 (95% CI −0.07, 0.37), p = 0.18; lower load (≤50% 1RM) ES = 0.28 (95% CI −0.06, 0.62), p = 0.11. Moderator effect not significant (p = 0.525), though the point-estimate gap (0.15 vs. 0.28) is described as providing "some support" for needing closer proximity-to-failure at lower loads.
- **Theme C, high (>25%) vs. moderate (20–25%) velocity loss:** trivial, non-significant. ES = 0.08 (95% CI −0.16, 0.32), p = 0.529. Q = 4.08, df = 10, p = 0.944, I² = 0% (6 studies, 11 effects).
- **Individual condition effect sizes** (pre- to post-intervention change, Table 4): Momentary muscular failure ES = 0.41 (95% CI 0.27, 0.55), p < 0.001; Theme A non-failure ES = 0.37 (95% CI 0.15, 0.58), p = 0.001; Set failure (Theme B) ES = 0.46 (95% CI 0.12, 0.80), p = 0.077; Theme B non-failure ES = 0.32 (95% CI 0.05, 0.60), p = 0.023; Low VL (<20%) ES = 0.20 (95% CI −0.02, 0.41), p = 0.072; Moderate VL (20–25%) ES = 0.39 (95% CI 0.09, 0.70), p = 0.010; High VL (>25%) ES = 0.42 (95% CI 0.12, 0.71), p = 0.005.
- Ordering these conditions from theoretically furthest to closest to failure (Figure 5) does **not** produce a strictly monotonic increase — "set failure" (0.46) numerically exceeds "momentary muscular failure" (0.41) — which the authors present as visual/conceptual evidence for a **non-linear** relationship, explicitly noting this ordering is exploratory, not a formally fitted non-linear model.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** the primary set-failure-vs-non-failure result (p = 0.045) assumed a pre-/post-test correlation coefficient of r = 0.75 (not directly reported in most source studies). Re-running with r = 0.6–0.9 shifted ES between 0.15–0.25 and p between 0.016–0.104 — i.e., statistical significance is not robust to this assumption.
- No publication bias detected (Egger's test, p < 0.05 threshold not met) for Theme A/B or Theme C.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This paper examines **muscle hypertrophy only**. Strength, power, and muscular endurance outcomes were explicitly excluded during screening (37 records excluded for "irrelevant outcomes"). It makes no claims about strength or endurance adaptations to proximity-to-failure.

## Practical interpretation

A cautious reading: literal momentary muscular failure does not appear necessary to maximize hypertrophy on average — non-failure conditions produced statistically similar hypertrophy to failure conditions in both Theme A and Theme B. However, because there is no consensus definition of "failure" or "non-failure" in the underlying literature, and because the true proximity-to-failure actually achieved by "non-failure" groups is poorly characterized (not directly measured in most studies), this null result should not be read as proof that proximity-to-failure is irrelevant — rather, that the specific comparisons tested so far are underpowered and definitionally noisy. The apparent non-linear pattern (Figure 5) is presented by the authors as conceptual/hypothesis-generating rather than a statistically confirmed non-linear model fit.

Authors' own practical-application discussion (Section 4.3, PDF p.14 — interpretive, not a direct statistical finding from this meta-analysis): RT prescription should not be treated dichotomously as "failure vs. non-failure" since sets can be a mix of both within a session. They suggest most sets be terminated with a close-but-submaximal proximity-to-failure to limit cumulative neuromuscular fatigue while preserving mechanical-tension exposure across multiple sets, and that the decision to occasionally push to momentary muscular failure should be biased toward: low-complexity/low-fatigue exercises (single-joint, machine-based, lower cardiovascular demand), the last set of an exercise/muscle group, muscle groups trained with low weekly volume (<5 sets/session) or low frequency (1–2×/week), resistance-trained (vs. untrained) individuals, and lower-load (vs. higher-load) sets. This guidance is the authors' own synthesis/opinion layered on top of the data, not itself a statistically tested finding of this review, and should be treated accordingly (i.e., as expert interpretation, not as an established causal result).

## Application relevance

- Supports treating "training to failure" and "not training to failure" as a loggable/trackable categorical or ordinal signal in a training app, but this paper does not itself provide a continuous RIR-based scale (unlike the companion Robinson meta-regression).
- Could inform a default assumption/message that literal failure is not required every set to drive hypertrophy — but the paper does not specify how far from failure is acceptable, so this cannot be turned into an exact rep/RIR cutoff.
- The volume-load/relative-load moderator analyses (non-significant but suggestive) could weakly inform a heuristic that lower-load, higher-rep work may benefit more from training closer to failure than heavier work — but this is explicitly non-significant (p = 0.525) and should not drive automated decisions.
- **Not suitable for automated decision-making** regarding a specific RIR/failure threshold; suitable only as descriptive/educational content given the trivial effect sizes, fragile significance under sensitivity analysis, and categorical (not continuous) exposure variable.

## What this paper DOES support

- No evidence that training to literal momentary muscular failure is superior to non-failure training for muscle hypertrophy (Theme A comparison, ES=0.12, ns).
- A trivial, borderline-significant overall advantage for "set failure" (any definition) vs. non-failure when definitions are pooled (ES=0.19, p=0.045) — but this is sensitive to analytic assumptions.
- No significant difference in hypertrophy between high (>25%) and moderate (20–25%) velocity-loss thresholds in trained individuals (ES=0.08, ns).
- Preliminary/conceptual support for a non-linear (not strictly monotonic) relationship between proximity-to-failure and hypertrophy.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- An exact RIR or velocity-loss percentage threshold that maximizes hypertrophy.
- Any claim about strength, power, or muscular endurance outcomes.
- A universal recommendation to always train to failure or to never train to failure.
- Firm confirmation of a non-linear dose-response shape — this is visually/conceptually proposed from six categorical point estimates with overlapping confidence intervals, not from a formally compared non-linear statistical model.
- Generalization of the velocity-loss findings (Theme C) to untrained populations, since all six Theme C studies used previously trained participants only.
- Generalization beyond short interventions (6–14 weeks) or to highly/elite strength-trained individuals.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- Small number of studies overall (15) and per theme (5, 4, and 6 studies respectively), limiting statistical power, particularly for sub-group/moderator analyses.
- No consensus definition of "failure" across the literature; "non-failure" comparator conditions vary widely in true achieved proximity-to-failure, which the authors state "pose[s] a challenge for deriving practical recommendations."
- Current set-termination control methods (e.g., velocity-loss thresholds) provide limited insight into the actual proximity-to-failure reached — cited evidence that only ~56% of participants training to a 40% velocity-loss threshold in one study actually reached momentary muscular failure.
- Pre-/post-test correlation coefficient was assumed (r=0.75) rather than observed for most studies; sensitivity analysis shows the primary result is not robust across the plausible r = 0.6–0.9 range (p ranged 0.016–0.104).
- Risk-of-bias concerns: none of the 15 studies described allocation concealment or included activity monitoring in control groups; only 1 study clearly reported an intention-to-treat analysis; 8 studies did not describe randomization procedures; several studies did not report % of participants withdrawing or number of completed sessions.
- Theme C (velocity-loss) evidence is restricted to previously trained participants only.
- Authors explicitly state results "are likely confounded by statistical power limitations, particularly in our sub-group analyses."

**Agent-assessed (not stated by authors):** The exposure variable in this review is inherently indirect — velocity-loss percentage and definitional category are used as proxies for "true" proximity-to-failure, and the authors' own cited evidence (the 56% figure above) shows this proxy is imperfect and highly variable between individuals at a given velocity-loss threshold. This compounds the well-known general difficulty of measuring/self-reporting proximity-to-failure accurately in resistance-training research.

## Confidence

**Low.** Although this is a peer-reviewed, PRISMA-guided systematic review with generally low between-study heterogeneity (I²=0% in most models) and reasonable methodological quality scores (TESTEX mean 9.9/15), the central finding is a *trivial* effect size whose statistical significance is fragile — it flips between significant and non-significant depending on an assumed (not observed) pre-/post-test correlation coefficient. Sub-group sample sizes are very small (4–6 studies per theme), the exposure variable (failure proximity) is categorically and indirectly defined with documented poor correspondence to true proximity-to-failure, and the proposed non-linear relationship is visual/conceptual rather than formally statistically modeled. This should not be treated as a firmly established causal dose-response relationship, though the systematic, low-heterogeneity, peer-reviewed nature of the review is a point in its favor relative to a purely exploratory analysis.

## Cross-reference note

This paper and the companion Robinson et al. (2023) preprint (see `robinson-proximity-to-failure.md`) both investigate proximity-to-failure and muscle hypertrophy and reach broadly compatible headline conclusions — training closer to failure trends toward greater hypertrophy, and literal momentary failure is not clearly superior to stopping somewhat short of it — but they diverge on the shape and strength of that relationship. Robinson finds a **continuous, linear, statistically robust** (CI excludes null in all four model variants) negative relationship between estimated RIR and hypertrophy across 26 studies/140 effects. Refalo instead finds only a **trivial, statistically fragile** (p flips with the assumed correlation coefficient) advantage for "set failure" over "non-failure" across 9 studies/18 effects, and proposes a **non-linear/plateauing** relationship based on six categorical point estimates rather than a fitted continuous model — a genuine disagreement in curve shape with Robinson's finding, not a restatement of it. Differences plausibly stem from methodology: Robinson pools a much larger and more heterogeneous set of studies (including velocity-loss and alternative-set-structure trials) using a continuous *estimated* RIR metric, while Refalo restricts to categorical failure/non-failure and velocity-loss-threshold comparisons in a smaller, more tightly themed set of studies with a directly measured (though still imperfect) velocity-loss proxy. Refalo is hypertrophy-only and does not address strength at all, whereas Robinson covers both outcomes and finds strength to be essentially unrelated to RIR — a question this paper is silent on. Neither paper is simply newer-and-superseding; they use different designs to probe overlapping but not identical evidence bases, and their disagreement on the linear-vs-non-linear shape of the hypertrophy relationship should be treated as an open question rather than resolved by either alone.
