## Evidence type

Systematic review + network meta-analysis (with univariate and multivariate network meta-regression). Included only experimental (interventional) studies of resistance training performed to volitional failure; not a purely observational review.

## Research question

To analyze the effect of resistance training (RT) performed until volitional failure with low, moderate, and high loads on muscle hypertrophy and muscle strength in healthy adults, and to assess possible participant-, design-, and training-related covariates that may affect these adaptations.

## Population and evidence base

- 28 studies included in the network meta-analysis; 747 healthy men and women total.
- Mean age 23.4 ± 3.0 yr (young adults; authors caution against extrapolating to other age groups).
- 19 of 28 studies (67.9%) involved predominantly men; 21 of 28 (75.0%) involved untrained participants; the remainder recreationally trained.
- No studies included highly strength-trained individuals (defined by the authors as average relative strength ≥2.0 body weights back squat or ≥1.5 body weights bench press).
- Study comparisons: 17 studies compared low- vs high-load, 4 compared low- vs moderate-load, 5 compared moderate- vs high-load, and 2 compared low- vs moderate- vs high-load.
- Mean intervention duration 8.9 ± 2.1 wk; mean 24.6 ± 7.5 sessions (range 16–48). No studies exceeded 14 wk.
- Between-subject design in 22/28 (78.6%); RT prescribed by repetitions maximum (RM) in 18/28 (64.3%).
- Hypertrophy outcome analyzed in 24 studies / 35 comparisons; strength outcome (1-RM) analyzed in 23 studies / 39 comparisons.

## Variables investigated

- **Load categories (as defined by this paper):** Low-load = <60% of 1-RM, or >15 RM. Moderate-load = 60–79% of 1-RM, or 9–15 RM. High-load = ≥80% of 1-RM, or ≤8 RM. All protocols performed to volitional failure.
- Outcomes: muscle hypertrophy (mass/size measures) and muscle strength (1-RM tests).
- Meta-regression covariates: year of publication, experimental design (between- vs within-subject), sex, training status (untrained vs recreationally trained), number of sessions, assessed limb (lower- vs upper-body), and prescription method (%1-RM vs RM).

## Main findings

**Hypertrophy (Table 2, Fig. 3):**
- High vs Low: overall SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.06 to 0.29), P = 0.241, k=19 comparisons, n=347; best-quality subgroup SMD 0.10 (95% CI −0.14 to 0.33), P = 0.871, k=12, n=274.
- Moderate vs Low: overall SMD 0.20 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.44), P = 0.113, k=7, n=128; best-quality SMD −0.06 (95% CI −0.54 to 0.42), P = 0.929, k=2, n=88.
- High vs Moderate: overall SMD −0.09 (95% CI −0.33 to 0.16), P = 0.469, k=9, n=107; best-quality SMD 0.15 (95% CI −0.34 to 0.65), P = 0.995, k=2, n=51.
- No significant differences in hypertrophy between any load pair, overall or in best-quality subgroup (P = 0.113–0.469 overall; 0.871–0.995 subgroup). I² = 0% (no heterogeneity); no significant inconsistency (Q = 6.2, P = 0.103 overall; Q = 0.3, P = 0.957 subgroup); no publication bias (Egger's P = 0.497–0.909).
- "Best load" probability: overall analysis favored moderate-load (84.5% probability); best-quality subgroup favored high-load (75.8%). Authors explicitly caveat these are "unlikely to be considered meaningful" given small effect sizes (range −0.09 to 0.15).

**Strength (Table 2, Fig. 4):** After adjustment for an outlier study (Anderson & Kearney) and publication-bias correction (Egger's P = 0.014 flagged bias pre-adjustment):
- High vs Low: SMD 0.60–0.63 across overall/best-quality analyses (95% CI 0.38–0.82 / 0.38–0.88), P < 0.001.
- Moderate vs Low: SMD 0.34–0.35 (95% CI 0.05–0.62 / 0.05–0.65), P = 0.002–0.003.
- High vs Moderate: SMD 0.26–0.28 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.58), nonsignificant, P = 0.066–0.068 ("approached statistical significance").
- Heterogeneity I² = 39.9% (overall) / 43.7% (subgroup); no publication bias in adjusted models (P = 0.277–0.350); inconsistency nonsignificant (Q = 5.3, P = 0.150 overall; node-splitting P = 0.588–0.892).
- Probability high-load is the best intervention for strength: 98.2% (both overall and best-quality subgroup).

**Meta-regression (Table 3):**
- Hypertrophy, High vs Low: training status (untrained vs recreationally trained) significant, coef −1.38 ± 0.65 (95% CI −2.67 to −0.11), P = 0.033 — untrained participants showed greater hypertrophy effects than recreationally trained.
- Hypertrophy, Moderate vs Low: number of sessions significant, coef 0.09 ± 0.04 (95% CI 0.01–0.16), P = 0.031 — more sessions associated with greater hypertrophy effect.
- Hypertrophy, High vs Moderate: number of sessions significant, coef 0.08 ± 0.04 (95% CI 0.01–0.15), P = 0.045.
- Strength, High vs Low: year of publication significant (coef −0.11 ± 0.05, P = 0.023, older studies showing larger effects) and sex significant (coef 1.03 ± 0.50, 95% CI 0.06–2.00, P = 0.037; men derived greater strength benefit than women in this comparison). Discussion text separately notes women showed relatively greater strength gains than men in moderate-load comparisons.
- No significant covariates for Moderate-vs-Low or High-vs-Moderate strength models.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

This is a central finding of the paper, stated explicitly in the abstract and discussion's "three important findings": (1) In untrained and recreationally trained individuals (non–strength-athletes), hypertrophy gains are likely similar regardless of RT load when training is performed to volitional failure over relatively short interventions — i.e., hypertrophy appeared **load-independent** in this dataset. (2) In direct contrast, muscle strength gains were **load-dependent**: higher loads (high > moderate > low) produced significantly greater 1-RM improvements over the same short interventions. (3) Both patterns were maintained when the analysis was restricted to higher-quality (lower risk-of-bias) studies. The authors attribute the strength finding to the principle of specificity (higher-load training more closely resembles the 1-RM test) combined with greater neural and skeletal-muscle adaptations from higher-load training.

## Practical interpretation

Author-stated practical application (verbatim intent): a high-load program (≥80% of 1-RM, or ≤8 RM) can target both hypertrophy and strength outcomes in shorter training periods; a moderate rep range (9–15 RM) is a reasonable alternative for those who cannot tolerate higher intensities, still eliciting hypertrophy and strength gains superior to low-load training. For those with prior training experience, undertaking more RT sessions is associated with superior hypertrophy gains regardless of load.

## Application relevance

- Trackable variables: load category (%1-RM or RM zone: low/<60%>15RM, moderate/60–79%|9–15RM, high/≥80%|≤8RM), training status (untrained vs. trained), and number of sessions/week.
- Recommendation signal: supports offering multiple load options (moderate or high) as valid hypertrophy pathways rather than prescribing one "correct" %1RM, while flagging high-load (or at least moderate-load) as preferable when strength (1-RM performance) is a stated user goal.
- Heuristic seed: "more sessions → better hypertrophy outcome for previously-trained users" and "load category matters more for strength goals than for hypertrophy goals" could seed a recommendation heuristic, but the underlying evidence is short-duration (≤14 wk) and should not be hardcoded as a permanent rule.
- Not suitable for automated decision-making regarding: precise rep-range prescriptions, long-term (>14 wk) periodization, or strength-trained/advanced lifter populations — none were represented in the source studies.

## What this paper DOES support

- That, to volitional failure, low/moderate/high loads produce statistically similar hypertrophy on average in untrained-to-recreationally-trained adults over short (~9 wk) interventions.
- That higher loads (high and moderate) produce significantly greater 1-RM strength gains than low loads over the same short interventions, with a load-dependent, dose-response-like gradient (high > moderate > low, though high vs moderate did not reach significance).
- That untrained participants show greater hypertrophy responses than recreationally trained participants, and that more RT sessions is associated with greater hypertrophy gains among those with prior training background.
- That these findings held when restricted to higher-quality (lower risk-of-bias) studies.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- A single universally optimal %1RM or RM zone for hypertrophy — the paper's own conclusion is that hypertrophy is largely load-independent within the ranges tested, not that one load is superior.
- Extrapolation to highly strength-trained individuals, bodybuilders, or competitive strength/power athletes — none were included (participants' relative strength was well below the paper's own "elite" threshold).
- Extrapolation beyond ~14 weeks of training, or to nonspecific strength/performance measures (isometric, isokinetic, muscular endurance) — these were explicitly excluded from the review.
- An exact minimum RM/load threshold that "maximizes" strength — the high- vs moderate-load strength comparison did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.066–0.068), so the paper cannot pinpoint a precise cutoff, only a directional trend favoring higher loads.
- A claim that load is irrelevant to hypertrophy in all populations or contexts — the finding is specific to volitional-failure training in this population/duration; the meta-regression shows training status and session count did modulate hypertrophy outcomes.
- Causal generalization beyond the between-group, largely between-subject, short-duration RCT designs analyzed — findings should be read as evidence from short-term controlled trials, not long-term real-world tracking data.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
1. Most included studies were low quality, primarily due to concerns about randomization/allocation concealment and outcome measurement (some concerns: 87.5% of hypertrophy studies, 87.0% of strength studies; high risk: 12.5% and 13.0% respectively). A best-quality subgroup analysis was used to mitigate this, with results largely maintained.
2. Nonspecific strength/performance measures (maximal voluntary contraction, muscular endurance) were excluded due to methodological variability; strength findings should not be extended to isometric/isokinetic strength tests.
3. Recreationally trained participants' strength levels were "relatively modest"; findings cannot be extrapolated to highly strength-trained individuals (bodybuilders, collegiate/structured strength-and-conditioning athletes).
4. Several studies performed repeated 1-RM testing (3–5 times) during the intervention, which may mask an even larger true difference between low- vs. moderate-/high-load strength gains, since repeated maximal testing itself can increase strength independent of the training intervention.
5. Muscle-size (hypertrophy) assessment is inherently challenged by load, movement pattern, range of motion, and contraction type, further complicated by measurement location/technology/methodology.
6. The evidence base is mostly young adults (23.4 ± 2.9 yr); results should be extrapolated cautiously to other populations, and volitional-failure training itself is not feasible/appropriate for all populations.

**Own interpretation (not stated by authors):** The "best intervention probability" percentages (e.g., 84.5% moderate-load for hypertrophy, 98.2% high-load for strength) come from a Bayesian-style ranking within the network model and should not be read as effect-size magnitude; the authors themselves flag the hypertrophy probability values as not practically meaningful given trivial SMDs. The short intervention duration (max ~14 wk, mean ~9 wk) also means these are early-adaptation-phase results and may not generalize to multi-month or multi-year training progressions relevant to an ongoing training app.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** This is a network meta-analysis of 28 experimental (RCT-like) studies with a reasonably large combined sample (747 participants) and load categories that were experimentally manipulated (not merely observed), which supports causal interpretation within the tested range. However, confidence is tempered by: most individual studies carrying "some concerns" or high risk of bias (largely randomization/allocation reporting); short intervention durations (mean 8.9 wk, max 14 wk) that limit generalizability to longer-term training; a population skewed toward untrained young adults with no strength-trained individuals; a flagged publication-bias signal in the raw strength network (corrected via outlier adjustment); and non-trivial heterogeneity in the strength model (I² = 39.9–43.7%) versus none in the hypertrophy model (I² = 0%). The hypertrophy "no difference between loads" finding is well-supported by consistent, low-heterogeneity data, while the strength "load-dependent" finding, though statistically clear for high/moderate vs. low, has a not-yet-significant high-vs-moderate contrast (P≈0.07-0.068) that should be treated as a trend rather than a confirmed threshold.
