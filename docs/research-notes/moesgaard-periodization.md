# Citation

Moesgaard L, Beck MM, Christiansen L, Aagaard P, Lundbye-Jensen J. "Effects of Periodization on Strength and Muscle Hypertrophy in Volume-Equated Resistance Training Programs: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis." *Sports Medicine*. Published online 19 January 2022. DOI: 10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1.

Source PDF:
`06_moesgaard_periodization.pdf`

## Evidence type

Systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled experimental resistance-training intervention studies (PRISMA-guided search of PubMed, Scopus, SPORTDiscus through 7 Feb 2021). Four separate random-effects (inverse-variance) meta-analyses were run on between-group effect sizes (ES) derived from within-group pre-post changes. Methodological quality of included studies was rated with a modified PEDro scale (max 8/10, since blinding items 5–6 are infeasible in exercise trials). Not a review of observational/cohort data — all comparisons come from prospective training interventions with parallel groups.

## Research question

(a) Does periodization of volume/intensity affect maximal strength (1RM) and muscle hypertrophy when total training volume is equated between periodized and non-periodized (NP) conditions? (b) Is any periodization effect influenced by training status (trained vs. untrained)? (c) How are volume and intensity most effectively periodized (linear [LP] vs. undulating [UP]) to maximize strength and hypertrophy?

## Population and evidence base

- 35 studies included (from 1103 screened, 81 full-text reviewed); 1187 total participants, with 1022 (129 adolescents <18y, 791 adults 18–49y, 102 older adults ≥50y) entering the meta-analyses after excluding non-resistance-training control arms and one study with unequated volume.
- Sex distribution reported for a subset of studies: 9 male-only, 7 female-only, 9 mixed-sex.
- Training status: 19 studies in untrained participants, 15 in trained participants, 1 in detrained participants. "Trained" was defined loosely by original authors, generally >6 months to >1 year of RT experience, with high within-study variation.
- Study duration: 6–36 weeks (mean 13.7 ± 6.0 weeks). Training frequency: 2–4 days/week (mean 2.9 ± 0.7). Prescribed volume: 1–7 sets/exercise, 1–30 reps. Intensity: 30–105% 1RM or 3–30RM.
- 15 of 35 studies reported compliance; mean compliance in those studies was 93.0 ± 10.4%.
- Methodological quality (modified PEDro): mean 4.8 ± 1.0 (range 2–7); 2 studies low quality (≤3), 24 moderate (4–5), 9 high (≥6).
- All included protocols equated total volume (sets × reps) and mean relative intensity between compared groups, and used identical frequency, exercises, exercise order.

## Variables investigated

Periodization models were operationally (re-)classified by the review authors, not simply taken from original study labels:
- **NP** — no manipulation of volume/intensity.
- **LP** ("traditional") — linear increase in intensity, linear decrease in volume over time.
- **RLP** (reverse linear) — linear decrease in intensity, linear increase in volume.
- **UP** (undulating/non-linear) — more frequent variation; subdivided into **DUP** (daily undulating) and **WUP** (weekly undulating).
- **BP** (block) — training divided into sequential blocks each targeting a distinct goal (e.g., a hypertrophy block followed by a strength block).

Four meta-analyses were performed: NP vs. periodized (1RM), NP vs. periodized (hypertrophy), LP vs. UP (1RM), LP vs. UP (hypertrophy). Other model comparisons (BP vs. UP, DUP vs. WUP, DUP vs. RLP, LP vs. RLP) had too few studies for meta-analysis and are reported narratively/per-study only.

## Main findings

**NP vs. periodized — 1RM strength** (15 studies, 10 untrained/5 trained, 70 between-group ES):
- Mean relative 1RM change: NP 22.7% ± 14.6% (1.77% ± 1.06%/week) vs. periodized 27.6% ± 18.5% (2.13% ± 1.32%/week). Mean within-group ES: NP 0.98 ± 0.70, periodized 1.30 ± 1.11.
- Meta-analysis: moderate heterogeneity (χ²=51.47, df=14, P<0.00001, I²=73%). Significant effect favoring periodized training: **ES 0.31, 95% CI [0.04, 0.57], Z=2.28, P=0.02**.
- Sensitivity analysis: removing one study (Monteiro et al.) reduced the effect to ES 0.19, 95% CI [0.01, 0.38], Z=2.05, P=0.04, and eliminated most heterogeneity (χ²=20.89, df=13, P=0.08, I²=38%). No other study was individually influential. No difference when trained/untrained analyzed separately.

**NP vs. periodized — muscle hypertrophy** (10 studies, 16 between-group ES):
- Mean relative muscle-mass change: NP 3.2% ± 3.2% (0.27% ± 0.31%/week) vs. periodized 4.2% ± 3.4% (0.34% ± 0.34%/week). Mean within-group ES: NP 0.22 ± 0.30, periodized 0.34 ± 0.28.
- Meta-analysis: no heterogeneity (χ²=3.73, df=9, P=0.93, I²=0%). No significant effect: **ES 0.13, 95% CI [–0.10, 0.36], Z=1.10, P=0.27**. No influential studies on sensitivity analysis; no difference by training status.

**LP vs. UP — 1RM strength** (14 studies assessed 1RM of 16 total, 9 untrained/6 trained/1 detrained, 38 between-group ES):
- Mean relative 1RM change: LP 17.8% ± 11.2% (1.71% ± 1.29%/week) vs. UP 22.8% ± 12.5% (2.20% ± 1.43%/week). Mean within-group ES: LP 0.81 ± 0.35, UP 1.26 ± 1.12.
- Meta-analysis: moderate heterogeneity (χ²=43.35, df=13, P<0.00001, I²=70%). Significant effect favoring UP: **ES 0.31, 95% CI [0.02, 0.61], Z=2.06, P=0.04**.
- Sensitivity analysis: removing Monteiro et al. halved the effect (ES 0.15, 95% CI [0.00, 0.30], Z=1.92, P=0.05) and eliminated heterogeneity (I²=0%, P=0.56).
- **Subgroup by training status**: trained participants — ES 0.61, 95% CI [0.00, 1.22], Z=1.97, P=0.05 (favors UP); untrained participants — ES 0.06, 95% CI [–0.20, 0.31], Z=0.43, P=0.67 (no difference). The overall LP-vs-UP effect was driven by trained individuals only.

**LP vs. UP — muscle hypertrophy** (11 studies, 16 between-group ES):
- Mean relative muscle-mass change: LP 4.4% ± 3.4% (0.37% ± 0.29%/week) vs. UP 4.9% ± 3.4% (0.46% ± 0.39%/week). Mean within-group ES: LP 0.34 ± 0.26, UP 0.38 ± 0.25.
- Meta-analysis: no heterogeneity (χ²=2.46, df=10, P=0.99, I²=0%). No significant difference: **ES 0.05, 95% CI [–0.20, 0.29], Z=0.36, P=0.72**. No influential studies; no difference by training status.

**Other model comparisons** (narrative only, no meta-analysis — insufficient study counts): 6 studies BP vs. UP (5 assessed hypertrophy), 4 studies DUP vs. WUP (2 assessed hypertrophy), 3 studies DUP vs. RLP (none assessed hypertrophy), 2 studies LP vs. RLP (neither assessed hypertrophy), 1 study LP vs. BP (1RM only). Most found no significant between-model differences in 1RM or hypertrophy; individual study values are in Table 1 of the paper but were not pooled.

**Context vs. prior meta-analyses**: Williams et al. (not volume-equated) found a larger periodized-vs-NP strength effect (ES 0.43); this review's smaller ES 0.31 with volume equated indicates the NP-vs-periodized strength advantage is not attributable to volume differences alone. Harries et al. previously found no LP-vs-UP strength difference; Caldas et al. found ES 0.22 favoring UP (broadly consistent with this review). Grgic et al.'s prior systematic reviews found no hypertrophy difference for NP-vs-periodized or LP-vs-UP, matching this review's conclusions.

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

The paper investigated **only two outcomes**: 1RM strength and muscle hypertrophy (via DXA, MRI, ultrasound, BIA, skinfolds, circumference, or BOD POD, depending on study). It does **not** report on muscular endurance, power, sport performance, or other outcomes.

- **Strength (1RM)**: Periodization (any model) > NP when volume equated (ES 0.31, P=0.02). UP > LP (ES 0.31, P=0.04), but this UP advantage over LP was significant only in trained participants (ES 0.61, P=0.05), not untrained (ES 0.06, P=0.67).
- **Hypertrophy**: No significant difference between NP and periodized training (ES 0.13, P=0.27), and no significant difference between LP and UP (ES 0.05, P=0.72), regardless of training status. The authors explicitly conclude periodization of volume/intensity "does not seem to affect muscle hypertrophy" in volume-equated pre-post designs.
- These are two separate causal claims from the same experimental base; the strength finding must not be generalized to hypertrophy, and vice versa — the paper itself frames this divergence as its central finding.

## Practical interpretation

The authors interpret the strength-but-not-hypertrophy divergence as evidence that periodization's benefit for maximal strength is likely mediated by neurophysiological adaptations (motor-unit recruitment, discharge rate, intermuscular coordination) rather than morphological (muscle-size) adaptations — since morphological change did not differ between periodized and non-periodized conditions but strength did. This is explicitly framed by the authors as speculative: "none of the studies investigating effects of periodization have investigated potential differences in neurophysiological adaptations, the underlying mechanisms need to be investigated in future studies." It is not a directly tested/measured finding.

For programming intent, the authors suggest: periodize (in any structured form) when the primary goal is maximal strength; for trained lifters specifically, favor daily/weekly undulation (UP) over linear progression (LP) for strength; periodization model choice appears to matter less (or not at all) for hypertrophy given equal volume. Two individual studies (Kraemer et al., De Souza et al.) hint that periodization effects may change over the course of a training block (e.g., NP/periodized groups converge early then diverge later), but the review states this time-course question is "beyond the scope of the present review" and unresolved.

## Application relevance

- **Trackable variable**: periodization-model classification (NP/LP/RLP/UP-DUP/UP-WUP/BP) and its consistency with prescribed volume/intensity trends over a mesocycle could be a program-design tag.
- **Recommendation signal (weak-to-moderate)**: for strength-focused blocks with trained users, some periodization structure is favored over a flat/static program, and daily-or-weekly intensity/volume undulation is favored over strict linear progression — but effect sizes are small (ES ~0.15–0.31 after sensitivity correction) and fragile to one influential study.
- **Recommendation signal (none)**: for hypertrophy-focused blocks, this paper gives no basis to prefer one periodization model over another, or over a non-periodized program, provided total volume is matched — periodization-model selection should not be presented as a hypertrophy lever.
- **Not suitable for automated decision-making**: a "best" periodization model cannot be auto-selected as universally correct — the strength effect is heterogeneous (I²=70–73%), collapses under removal of one study, and (for LP vs. UP) applies only to trained users. There is no basis in this paper for a mandatory mesocycle length (durations studied ranged 6–36 weeks with no length singled out as necessary or optimal).
- **Heuristic seed only**: "trained lifters may benefit more from frequent (daily/weekly) load/volume variation than from strict linear progression, for strength goals specifically" — presentable as a soft suggestion, not a rule.

## What this paper DOES support

- When training volume is equated, periodized resistance training (any structured model, pooled) produces a small but statistically significant advantage over non-periodized training for 1RM strength gains.
- When training volume is equated, undulating periodization (UP) produces a small but statistically significant 1RM strength advantage over linear periodization (LP), and this advantage is specifically attributable to trained participants.
- Muscle hypertrophy outcomes (as measured in these 35 studies) are not meaningfully different between periodized and non-periodized programs, nor between LP and UP, when volume is equated.
- These are experimentally derived (interventional, controlled) findings, not observational associations.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- A mandatory or optimal mesocycle/training-block length — none was tested or recommended.
- One periodization model being universally superior for hypertrophy — no model comparison showed a hypertrophy difference.
- Undulating periodization being superior to linear periodization for untrained individuals specifically — the UP-vs-LP strength advantage was not significant in untrained participants (ES 0.06, P=0.67).
- Any claim about muscular endurance, power, or sport-performance outcomes — these were not measured.
- A confirmed neurophysiological mechanism for the strength effect — this is the authors' speculative interpretation, not a measured outcome in the review.
- Generalization to highly trained/elite athletes — most "trained" participants had only ~6 months to a few years of experience.
- A definitive ranking among BP, DUP, WUP, and RLP models — too few studies existed for meta-analysis on these comparisons.

## Limitations

**Author-stated:**
- Hypertrophy was mostly assessed with indirect methods (skinfolds, BIA, circumference) rather than gold-standard MRI; even MRI may lack sensitivity to detect small hypertrophy differences, so the null hypertrophy finding could reflect measurement insensitivity rather than a true absence of effect.
- "Trained" status was inconsistently defined across the 35 source studies (commonly >6 months to >1 year of experience, with large within-study variation), limiting applicability to highly trained/elite populations.
- Numerous between-study variables besides periodization model (exercise selection, %1RM/RM range used, proximity to failure/effort) were not controlled for and could confound the isolated "periodization" effect.
- High inter-individual variability in training response is known in the literature; a periodization model shown superior on average may not be superior for a given individual, and individual responsiveness may change over time — the authors state findings should be applied only as "a general recommendation."
- Study durations (6–36 weeks) are short relative to real long-term athlete macrocycles; whether periodization's effect grows or shrinks over longer periods is unresolved (two individual studies point in different directions on this).
- Insufficient study counts prevented meta-analysis of BP-vs-UP, DUP-vs-WUP, DUP-vs-RLP, and LP-vs-RLP comparisons.

**Reviewer's (this note's) interpretation, not stated by the authors:**
- The review authors applied their own operational definitions to classify each study's design into NP/LP/RLP/UP/BP categories rather than relying on the original studies' self-labeled periodization terminology — this improves internal consistency for pooling but means the "periodization model" label is a post-hoc reclassification, and definitional inconsistency in the primary literature (which the authors implicitly address by imposing their own taxonomy) is a background risk for this evidence base generally.
- Two of the four meta-analyses showing significant effects (NP-vs-periodized 1RM, LP-vs-UP 1RM) are substantially dependent on a single study (Monteiro et al.) — removing it roughly halves the effect size in both cases, which suggests the "significant" findings are not robust to the underlying study set and should be treated cautiously.
- No funnel plot, Egger's test, or other formal publication-bias assessment was reported in the text.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** This is a PRISMA-guided random-effects meta-analysis with a reasonably large evidence base (35 studies, ~1022 analyzed participants) and volume-equated experimental designs, which is a methodological strength relative to prior periodization meta-analyses. However, confidence is tempered by: (1) moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I²=70–73%) in both significant strength analyses, substantially explained by a single influential study whose removal roughly halves the effect size; (2) modest average source-study quality (mean PEDro 4.8/8, only 9/35 rated high quality); (3) hypertrophy outcomes measured mostly by indirect, lower-precision methods, making the null hypertrophy finding harder to interpret as true equivalence versus a false negative; (4) loosely and inconsistently defined "trained" status across source studies; (5) no formal publication-bias assessment reported; and (6) small absolute effect sizes (ES 0.13–0.31) with confidence intervals that in several cases only narrowly exclude zero. The hypertrophy null findings (I²=0%, consistent, non-significant across both comparisons) are comparatively more stable/trustworthy than the strength findings, which are directionally consistent but statistically fragile.
