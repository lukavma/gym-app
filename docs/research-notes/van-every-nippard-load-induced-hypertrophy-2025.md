# Citation

Van Every DW, Lees MJ, Wilson B, Nippard J, Phillips SM. Load-induced human skeletal muscle hypertrophy: Mechanisms, myths, and misconceptions. *Journal of Sport and Health Science* 2026;15:101104. Received 29 July 2025; revised 23 September 2025; accepted 8 October 2025; available online 21 November 2025. DOI: 10.1016/j.jshs.2025.101104. Open access (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0).

Source PDF:
`10_van_every_nippard_load_induced_hypertrophy_2025.pdf`

## Evidence type

Narrative review (not a systematic review or meta-analysis in its own right; no stated systematic search protocol, PRISMA flow, or formal risk-of-bias/GRADE rating). It synthesizes human resistance-exercise-training (RET) intervention studies, some rodent and in-vitro/cell-culture mechanistic studies, and cites at least one external systematic review/meta-analysis (Benito et al. 2020, 111 studies) and one external dose-response meta-regression (Pelland et al. 2025) for specific numeric estimates.

## Research question

To critically evaluate several long-standing proposed mechanisms/claims about what drives RET-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy — specifically the roles of (1) acute/chronic systemic anabolic hormones, (2) metabolic stress (metabolite accumulation), (3) cell swelling ("the pump"), and (4) sarcoplasmic hypertrophy — against the evidence for mechanical tension (via mechanotransduction) as the primary driver. Secondarily, to characterize realistic short- and long-term expectations ("the upper limit") for muscle mass gain from RET.

## Population and evidence base

Not a single defined population — the review draws on a heterogeneous mix of sources:
- Human RET intervention studies, sample sizes ranging from n=7 (Trappe et al., older untrained males/females) to n=56 (West & Phillips correlational cohort) to n=951 (Benito et al. meta-analysis, 61 studies/82 groups, males only, age 24±3 years, 79.5±6.4 kg, 178±4 cm; RET duration 4–24 weeks, average 10.4±5.4 weeks).
- Training status varies by cited study: untrained/recreationally active (most common), previously-trained (e.g., Vann et al., n=15, prior RET experience), and one cohort of highly competitive bodybuilders (Alway et al., n=10, 24 weeks).
- Some conclusions rest on rodent models (e.g., mouse plantaris mechanical overload, treadmill running with oral lactate/caffeine) and in-vitro/cell culture models (e.g., HEK293T cells, hepatocytes, myoblast cultures) rather than human tissue.
- The paper explicitly notes most RET research is limited to 8–12 weeks (an academic-semester duration) and that longitudinal (multi-year) RET adaptation research is scarce.
- Sex: most cited hypertrophy-mechanism studies are male-only; sex-comparison data are drawn from separate meta-analyses (not detailed further) and one primary MPS comparison study (n unreported here).

## Variables investigated

Mechanical tension/mechanotransduction (integrins, focal adhesion kinase, titin, phosphatidic acid–YAP/TAZ–Hippo signaling); systemic hormones (testosterone, growth hormone, IGF-1, cortisol) — acute and chronic; metabolites (lactate, inorganic phosphate, hydrogen ions) and protein lactylation; cell swelling/hyperemia ("the pump"); sarcoplasmic vs. myofibrillar hypertrophy (fiber cross-sectional area [CSA] partitioning); load/%1RM (briefly — hypertrophy across "a wide range of loads," single citation, not a dose-response analysis in this paper); training volume; and long-term/upper-limit fat-free mass (FFM) response variability.

## Main findings

- **Mechanical tension**: positioned as the primary driver; the review states hypertrophy "can be obtained across a wide range of loads" (p. 2, citing ref. 26) but does not itself present a %1RM dose-response comparison.
- **Hormones**: Wilkinson et al. — unilateral RET, 10 young males, 8 weeks, 3x/week: no change in systemic T/GH/IGF-1/cortisol; trained-leg muscle CSA +22%, type IIx fiber CSA +22%, type IIa +13% (p<0.05); leg press strength +25 kg, knee extension +21 kg (p<0.05); untrained leg unchanged (p. 3). West et al. — acute high-hormone (HH) vs. low-hormone (LH) unilateral elbow-flexor protocol: HH produced 4–5 fold higher hormones but no difference in MPS or p70S6K phosphorylation (p. 4). Chronic 15-week follow-up: LH arm +12%±2% vs. HH arm +10%±2% CSA (p>0.05) despite persistently greater HH exposure (p. 4). West & Phillips, n=56: hormone rises not significantly correlated with lean-mass or strength gains (p. 4). Sex: 10–20 fold lower total T and up to 200-fold lower free T in females did not translate to lower relative muscle/strength gains per cited meta-analyses; no sex difference in 24-h post-RET MPS (p. 4).
- **Metabolites**: bodybuilding-style set (12 reps biceps curls) elevated intramuscular lactate to 91 mmol/kg (dry weight), rising to 118 mmol/kg after 3 sets (resting ~5 mmol/kg) (p. 5). Mattingly et al.: 8 sets exhaustive lower-body RET raised lactate ~7.2-fold (p<0.001) with no change in protein lactylation or lactylation-responsive genes; 6 weeks RT increased vastus lateralis (VL) size +3.5% (p=0.04) without lactylation changes (p. 5). Lactate-infusion crossover: blood lactate ~130% higher with infusion (3.0 vs. 7.0 mmol/L, p<0.001), intramuscular only modestly higher (27 vs. 32 mmol/kg, p=0.003), with no difference in pH, mTOR/S6K1/p44 phosphorylation, or 24-h fractional synthesis rate (p. 5–6). Mouse mechanical-overload model: +43.7% plantaris hypertrophy (p<0.001) with no difference in cytoplasmic lactylation (p=0.369) and reduced nuclear lactylation vs. sham (p<0.001) (p. 6). Bergamasco et al.: BFR+80%1RM vs. 80%1RM alone — BFR produced greater metabolic-stress markers but no difference in hypertrophy (p. 6).
- **Cell swelling/"the pump"**: Hirono et al., 6 weeks: correlations between acute post-exercise muscle swelling and subsequent hypertrophy were r=0.441, 0.550, 0.547, 0.475 at 0/5/10/15 min (~25% of variance) — correlational only (p. 7). Oda et al. (abstract only, not peer-reviewed full paper): r=0.682 between swelling and hypertrophy after 8 weeks of 100-rep protocol (p. 7). Van Vossel et al.: fast-twitch-dominant individuals showed 37–60% greater post-exercise femoral blood-flow AUC than slow-twitch individuals across 40–80%1RM (largest at 60%1RM), but this did not translate into greater long-term hypertrophy when both groups trained to failure at the same relative load (p. 7).
- **Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**: mixed/largely unsupportive evidence. MacDougall et al. (6 months, untrained males): type II fiber CSA increased alongside a 3% reduction (p<0.05) in myofibril-occupied space and ~15% increase in sarcoplasmic volume (p. 7). Haun et al. (6-week high-volume program, 31 men, subset of 15 with ~23% fCSA increase): omnibus ANOVA for fCSA not significant (p=0.152); myosin/actin concentration changes not significant (p=0.052/0.055); a forced post hoc analysis suggested ~30% decreases in myosin/actin, which the review flags as questionable practice; removing one outlier participant pushed the myosin/actin p-value from 0.035 to 0.053 (p. 7–8). By contrast, Trappe et al. (older untrained males, 12 weeks), Widrick et al. (12 weeks), Roberts (12 weeks, 13 high-responders: +34% mean fCSA, +24% VL thickness), and Vann et al. (10 weeks, +19% type II fCSA) all report proportional myofibrillar accretion with no disproportionate sarcoplasmic expansion (p. 8–9). Tracer studies (Moore et al.; Wilkinson et al., D2O; Brook et al.) consistently show myofibrillar protein synthesis rising robustly with RET while sarcoplasmic protein synthesis responds more to nutrient provision than to the contractile stimulus (p. 9). Jorgenson et al., using a novel imaging method (FIM-ID) in animal and human models, found radial muscle-fiber growth is largely mediated by myofibrillogenesis (p. 9).
- **Upper limit of hypertrophy**: Kraemer et al.: 11% arm CSA increase in untrained women over the first 3 months, declining to 6% over the next 3 months (p. 10). Alway et al.: 10 competitive bodybuilders, 24 weeks — no significant change in muscle fiber size (p. 10). Quadriceps thickness typically increases 0.10–0.25 cm per training intervention (some reports up to 0.72 cm over 8 weeks) (p. 10). Benito et al. (2020 meta-analysis, 111 studies, 61 studies/82 groups/951 male participants): average FFM gain 1.53 kg (95%CI: 1.30–1.76 kg) over interventions averaging 10.4±5.4 weeks; untrained gained numerically more than trained (untrained 1.54 kg, 95%CI 1.12–1.96 vs. trained 0.98 kg, 95%CI 0.17–1.79), not statistically significant (p. 10). Bottom-10% responders ~0.44 kg; one study's top responder +5.62 kg (p. 10–11). With anabolic steroids, Bhasin et al. reported a mean +6.1 kg FFM after 10 weeks of 600 mg/week exogenous testosterone (p. 11). Natural bodybuilder FFMI range 22.7–25.0, ~2.5–4.0 kg/year average over a 5-year career (p. 11). A case-study pro bodybuilder: 184 cm, 131.4 kg at 12.2% body fat, FFMI 34.1 (p. 11).

## Strength / hypertrophy distinction

The review is explicitly hypertrophy-focused. Strength data appear only where the source studies reported them alongside hypertrophy (e.g., Wilkinson et al.'s leg press/knee extension gains, West et al.'s strength outcomes). The review's own conceptual model (Fig. 3, p. 10) explicitly separates the two processes: early strength gains are attributed primarily to neural/skill acquisition, with hypertrophy's contribution to force capacity growing "more significant over time" before plateauing. The paper states muscle fiber hypertrophy is "contributory to strength changes" but notes "this thesis has been challenged," i.e., it does not claim a fixed, quantified relationship between the hypertrophy findings reported and strength/endurance outcomes. No endurance-training-relevant claims are made.

## Practical interpretation

The authors state RET programming should emphasize a "personalized, goal-dependent approach with relatively high effort" — proximity to failure (the paper specifies "~2 reps in reserve" as their example), sufficient volume, and progressive overload (increases in load, reps, and/or volume) to maintain mechanical tension. They state hypertrophy can be achieved via "a wide range of loading paradigms," so load selection can be tailored to preference/lifestyle. They explicitly state that strategies designed to transiently elevate hormones or metabolic stress "offer little additional benefit for hypertrophy," and that pursuing "the pump" does not affect long-term muscular adaptations (though may aid adherence if enjoyable). Adherence, volume, frequency, and intensity are described as "by far the most important factors" (p. 12).

## Application relevance

- Trackable variables consistent with this paper: training volume (sets/session or /week), load progression over time, proximity to failure (as reps-in-reserve, used descriptively by the authors, not as a validated RP construct), session frequency, and longitudinal body-composition/FFM tracking against realistic short-term (weeks) vs. long-term (months–years) expectancy curves.
- The FFM response-variability figures (Fig. 4; average, high, low responders by sex) could seed a "realistic expectations" display, but the review itself cautions these are schematic/illustrative estimates built from indirect calculations, not a validated predictive model — not suitable for precise individual-level automated prediction.
- Not suitable for automated decision-making: specific %1RM thresholds, sarcoplasmic-vs-myofibrillar partitioning, or hormone/metabolite/pump-based programming rules — the paper argues these should NOT be used as levers in program design.

## What this paper DOES support

- Mechanical tension (via mechanotransduction) as the primary recognized driver of load-induced hypertrophy.
- That acute and short-term chronic elevations in systemic anabolic hormones (T, GH, IGF-1) within physiological (non-exogenous) ranges are not necessary for, nor meaningfully correlated with, RET-induced hypertrophy, based on multiple converging human intervention studies.
- That metabolite accumulation (lactate, Pi, H+) is not shown to independently/causally drive hypertrophy in the human and rodent studies reviewed, beyond a possible indirect role in fatigue-driven motor unit recruitment.
- That acute muscle swelling/hyperemia ("the pump") does not reliably predict or drive long-term hypertrophy beyond weak, non-causal correlations in small human studies.
- That hypertrophy is achievable across a wide range of training loads (stated, not quantified in this paper).
- That average FFM gains in the reviewed meta-analytic data (Benito et al.) are on the order of ~1.5 kg over ~10 weeks in predominantly untrained males, with wide individual variability.

## What this paper DOES NOT support

- A single universally optimal load (%1RM) for hypertrophy — the paper only states a wide range of loads can work; it presents no dose-response curve or comparative effect sizes across specific %1RM bands.
- That sarcoplasmic hypertrophy is a well-established, major contributor to muscle growth in trained individuals — evidence is mixed, and the most supportive human study (Haun et al.) had a non-significant primary statistical test and a flagged post hoc analysis.
- Precise, generalizable numeric "genetic ceilings" for individual muscle growth — Forbes' theoretical FFM limits (100 kg males/60 kg females) are explicitly described as refuted by more recent data.
- That mechanistic/molecular-signaling findings (many from rodent or cell-culture models) directly and precisely translate into human hypertrophy outcomes or programming thresholds — the review itself treats these as indirect, supporting evidence rather than proof.
- Causal claims from the correlational swelling-hypertrophy or metabolite-hypertrophy studies — the authors themselves state causation "is difficult to decipher" in these designs.

## Limitations

Author-stated:
- Most RET intervention research is limited to 8–12 weeks, capturing only the initial portion of the adaptation curve, not long-term/multi-year trajectories (p. 10).
- Small sample sizes are common across the underlying human studies (e.g., n=7, n=10, n=15), and the authors themselves note this increases the risk of false-positive findings (explicitly discussed for the Haun et al. sarcoplasmic hypertrophy study, p. 8).
- "Weak evidence to quantify inter-individual variation in RET responses for appropriate categorization" is explicitly acknowledged (p. 10).
- Significant knowledge gaps regarding confirmation of sarcoplasmic hypertrophy in humans are explicitly acknowledged (p. 9).
- One key supporting study (Oda et al., cell-swelling correlation) has only been published as a conference abstract, not a full peer-reviewed paper (p. 7).
- Authors disclose competing interests: DWVE is a fitness-industry coach; JN is director of STRCNG Incorporated o/a Jeff Nippard Fitness (a commercial coaching/education brand); both state no involvement in study design or manuscript decisions. SMP discloses research funding from multiple food/supplement industry sources and patents licensed to a commercial entity (p. 12).

My own interpretation (not stated by authors):
- This is a narrative, not systematic, review — no described search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or risk-of-bias grading, so study selection could be subject to the authors' judgment/selection bias.
- A substantial share of the mechanistic case (Hippo/YAP-TAZ signaling, phosphatidic acid pathways, some lactate/myogenesis findings, mouse mechanical-overload lactylation data) rests on rodent or in-vitro/cell-culture models rather than directly measured human hypertrophy — this is an indirectness limitation the review does not always flag explicitly when drawing conclusions for human RET practice.
- Despite the paper's title emphasizing "load-induced" hypertrophy, load/%1RM dose-response is addressed only in a single sentence with one citation — the paper's actual empirical weight is on ruling out non-tension mechanisms (hormones, metabolites, swelling, sarcoplasmic expansion) and on characterizing long-term FFM-gain expectations, not on mapping load-response relationships.

## Confidence

**Moderate**, with meaningful internal variation by claim. Confidence is comparatively higher for the core claims that acute/chronic hormonal fluctuations and cell swelling do not drive hypertrophy, since these rest on multiple converging, directly-measured human RET intervention studies with hypertrophy or MPS as endpoints (e.g., Wilkinson et al., West et al., West & Phillips). Confidence is lower for the sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy discussion, where the key supportive study had non-significant/borderline statistics and a flagged post hoc analysis, and for the metabolite-mechanism conclusions, which combine rodent/in-vitro data with a smaller number of human studies. Confidence is also limited by: the narrative (non-systematic) review format with no formal bias assessment; frequent reliance on small human samples (n=7–31) and short (8–12 week) interventions; partial reliance on indirect/mechanistic and animal evidence for signaling-pathway claims; and disclosed author conflicts of interest (commercial coaching affiliations, industry funding) that do not appear to bias the specific claims evaluated here but warrant note. The load/%1RM dose-response angle implied by this paper's title is barely addressed and should not be treated as a validated finding of this specific paper.
